    Chapter 11 — Acquisition of the Syntax-Discourse & Semantics-Pragmatics Interfaces #

    Anchor text: Slabakova, R. (2016). Second Language Acquisition. Chapter 11: Acquisition of the Syntax–Discourse and Semantics–Pragmatics Interfaces.
    Link to Chapter 11

    ⭐ Overview #

    • The two key external interfaces:
      1. Syntax–Discourse Interface: How syntax encodes discourse information (e.g., Topic/Focus via word order, clitics) .
      2. Semantics–Pragmatics Interface: How context and universal pragmatic principles (e.g., Gricean maxims) adjust literal meaning (e.g., scalar implicatures) .
    • Why interface properties are often harder to acquire: They require integrating linguistic (syntax/semantics) and extralinguistic (context/culture) information—taxing working memory .
    • Key phenomena: Topic/Focus marking (clitic doubling, word order), pronoun reference (null/overt pronoun division of labor), intonation as discourse cue, and scalar implicatures .

    🎯 Learning goals #

    By the end of Week 11 you should be able to:

    1. Distinguish the syntax–discourse interface (e.g., clitic doubling for Topic) from the semantics–pragmatics interface (e.g., scalar implicatures) and explain why both rely on context .
    2. Describe how languages mark Topic/Focus (e.g., English topicalization vs. Spanish clitic left dislocation) and predict L2 acquisition challenges .
    3. Apply the Interface Hypothesis to explain why near-native learners struggle with pronoun reference (e.g., Italian overt pronouns for Topic shift) .
    4. Explain why scalar implicatures (e.g., some = “not all”) are universal but may still be processed differently in L2—especially under cognitive load .

    CHAPTER 11: ACQUISITION OF THE SYNTAX–DISCOURSE & SEMANTICS–PRAGMATICS INTERFACES #

    Core Goal: Understand how L2 learners integrate syntax/semantics with discourse context and pragmatic principles—and why these interfaces are uniquely challenging.

    11.1 Defining the Interfaces #

    External interfaces connect core grammar to context—unlike internal interfaces (e.g., syntax–semantics) which are language-internal:

    InterfaceCore FunctionKey Phenomena
    Syntax–DiscourseEncodes discourse status (Topic = old info; Focus = new info) via syntaxClitic doubling, topicalization, VS word order, pronoun reference
    Semantics–PragmaticsAdjusts literal (semantic) meaning via context/world knowledgeScalar implicatures, deixis (this/that), anaphoric deaccenting
    • Key Distinction: Syntax–discourse relies on prior discourse (e.g., “The wine” = Topic because it was mentioned earlier); semantics–pragmatics relies on universal principles (e.g., Grice’s Quantity Maxim) .

    11.2 Marking Topic & Focus Across Languages #

    Languages use different syntactic strategies to signal Topic (old info) and Focus (new info)—mismatches between L1 and L2 create acquisition challenges:

    1. Clitic Doubling for Topic #

    • Spanish/Bulgarian: Topicalized objects require a clitic pronoun (doubling) to signal old info. Example:
      • Spanish: A Pedro, no lo saludó (“Pedro, I didn’t greet him”) → lo = clitic doubling Topic Pedro .
      • Bulgarian: *Vinoto tja ne (go) pi (“The wine she didn’t drink it”) → go = mandatory clitic for Topic vinoto .
    • English: Topicalized objects forbid resumptive pronouns: *The wine she didn’t drink (it) .
    • Acquisition Findings:
      • Advanced English learners of Bulgarian master clitic doubling for Topic (Ivanov 2009) .
      • Spanish learners of English struggle to omit resumptive pronouns in topicalization (Slabakova & García Mayo 2015)—due to English topicalization’s rarity in input .

    2. Word Order for Focus #

    • Spanish (null-subject language): VS word order signals presentational Focus (new info):
      • Q: ¿Quién gritó? (“Who shouted?”) → A: Gritó una mujer (“Shouted a woman”) .
      • Neutral context (out of blue): VS preferred for unaccusative verbs (Vino la policía = “Arrived the police”) but not unergative verbs (Una mujer gritó = “A woman shouted”) .
    • English: Fixed SV order; Focus marked via intonation (e.g., A WOMAN shouted) .
    • Acquisition Findings:
      • English learners of Spanish master SV/VS distinction for unaccusative/unergative verbs but fail to link VS to Focus (Lozano 2006)—supports the Interface Hypothesis (discourse integration is taxing) .

    11.3 Pronoun Reference at the Syntax–Discourse Interface #

    Null and overt pronouns have a “division of labor” in discourse—this is a classic interface property:

    1. Romance Languages (Spanish/Italian) #

    • Null Pronouns: Signal Topic maintenance (embedded subject = main clause subject). Example:
      • Italian: La vecchietta saluta la ragazza quando attraversa la strada (“The old lady greets the girl when [she] crosses the street”) → attraversa = null pronoun = old Topic (la vecchietta) .
    • Overt Pronouns: Signal Topic shift (embedded subject = new referent). Example:
      • Italian: La vecchietta saluta la ragazza quando lei attraversa la strada (“The old lady greets the girl when she crosses the street”) → lei = overt pronoun = new referent (la ragazza) .

    2. L2 Acquisition Challenges #

    • Near-Native Learners: Less sensitive to Topic shift—30% of Italian near-natives interpret overt pronouns as Topic maintenance (vs. 5% of natives) (Belletti et al. 2007) .
    • Heritage Speakers: May never acquire the division of labor (Shin & Cairns 2009)—exposed to English before age 7 (when Topic shift sensitivity emerges in L1) .
    • Chinese Learners of English: Master null object reference (e.g., Xiao Zhang says Lao Wang likes ø) by advanced proficiency—showing interface success is possible with clear input .

    11.4 Intonation as a Discourse Cue #

    Intonation (nuclear stress, deaccenting) signals Focus/Topic—but L1 prosodic rules transfer and complicate L2 learning:

    1. English vs. Spanish Prosody #

    • English: Nuclear Stress (NS) is flexible—marks Focus on any constituent. Example:
      • Q: Who broke his leg? → A: A BOY broke his leg (NS on Focus boy) .
      • Anaphoric Deaccenting: Old info loses stress (e.g., Q: Why buy that stamp? → A: Because I COLLECT stamps—NS shifts to collect because stamps is old info) .
    • Spanish: NS is fixed (last constituent) — Focus marked via word order (not intonation) .

    2. Acquisition Findings #

    • Spanish learners of English acquire deaccenting (new rule) before restructuring NS (L1 transfer) (Nava & Zubizarreta 2009) .
    • Advanced learners still struggle with NS flexibility—prefer L1-like fixed stress (Zubizarreta & Nava 2011) .

    11.5 Scalar Implicatures at the Semantics–Pragmatics Interface #

    Scalar implicatures (e.g., some = “not all”) are universal but require cognitive effort—explaining L2 processing differences:

    1. How Implicatures Work #

    • Logical Meaning: Some professors are smart = “some and possibly all” .
    • Pragmatic Meaning: Some professors are smart = “not all professors are smart” (via Grice’s Quantity Maxim: “Be as informative as required”) .

    2. L2 Acquisition Findings #

    • Universal Mechanisms: Korean learners of English compute implicatures at 60% (vs. natives’ 40%) in L2—better than L1 children (Slabakova 2010) .
    • Processing Load: Learners struggle with indirect implicatures (e.g., Max didn’t read all the books = “Max read some”)—more cognitively demanding than direct implicatures (Lieberman 2009) .
    • Bilingual Advantage: Bilingual children outperform monolinguals on Gricean maxims (relevance, politeness) but not on scalar implicatures—suggests different processing demands (Siegal et al. 2009) .

    11.6 The Interface Hypothesis & Key Conclusions #

    • Interface Hypothesis (Sorace 2011): External interfaces (syntax–discourse, semantics–pragmatics) are more vulnerable than internal interfaces—due to working memory strain from integrating linguistic/contextual info .
    • Nuance: Interface difficulty is not universal—frequency of constructions matters:
      • Spanish clitic left dislocation (common) is acquired easily; English topicalization (rare) is not (Slabakova 2015) .
    • Final Takeaways:
      1. Syntax–discourse properties require matching L2 syntax to discourse context—challenged by L1 transfer and input rarity .
      2. Semantics–pragmatics properties (implicatures) are universal but taxed by L2 processing load .
      3. Near-native learners may show residual optionality at interfaces—but success is possible with sufficient input .
