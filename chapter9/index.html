    Chapter 9 — ACQUISITION OF THE MENTAL LEXICON #

    Anchor text: Slabakova, R. (2016). Second Language Acquisition. Chapter 9: Acquisition of the Mental Lexicon.
    Link to Chapter 9

    1. Pre-Class Preview Guide #

    1.1 Chapter Core Focus #

    This chapter explores how the mental lexicon (the brain’s storage system for words) works in bilinguals, covering key topics like how L1/L2 words are stored and retrieved, how bilinguals inhibit one language to speak another, how complex words are decomposed, and how learners acquire word meaning extensions (e.g., metonymy).

    1.2 Key Learning Objectives #

    By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

    1. Distinguish between the Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM) and BIA+ Model of bilingual lexicon representation.
    2. Explain how bilinguals inhibit non-target languages (Green’s Inhibitory Control Model) and the cognitive effects of this inhibition.
    3. Analyze how L2 learners decompose polymorphemic words (e.g., worked = work + -ed) using Ullman’s Declarative/Procedural Model.
    4. Identify challenges in acquiring verb argument structure (e.g., causative-inchoative alternations) when L1 and L2 differ.
    5. Interpret transfer of reference (metonymy) in L2 and explain why regular metonyms are easier to acquire than novel ones.

    1.3 Preview Questions (To Guide Reading) #

    • How do beginner vs. advanced bilinguals differ in linking L2 words to concepts (e.g., cat → “cat”)?
    • Why do bilinguals take longer to switch from L2 back to L1 (e.g., Spanish → English → Spanish)?
    • Do L2 learners store worked as a whole word or decompose it into work + -ed? How does proficiency affect this?
    • What errors do Chinese L1 learners of English make with psychological verbs (e.g., disappoint)?
    • Why do advanced L2 learners understand read Dickens (regular metonymy) but struggle with read Needham (novel metonymy)?

    Section 9.1: Mental Representation and Access of Lexical Items #

    Key Takeaways #

    Bilinguals’ mental lexicon is integrated (L1 and L2 words stored together), not separate. Lexical access is language-nonspecific (both languages activate at once), and two models explain representation: RHM (mediated L2-concept links for beginners) and BIA+ (feature-based activation across L1/L2).

    Core Concepts & Evidence #

    1. Foundational Terms: Lexeme vs. Lemma

      • Lexeme: Stores formal properties (spelling, pronunciation) — e.g., cat /kæt/, cats /kæts/.
      • Lemma: Stores semantic (meaning) and syntactic (word class) properties — e.g., cat = [+noun, +animate, “small feline”].
      • L1 children acquire lemmas first; lexemes develop with repeated exposure.

    2. Bilingual Lexicon Models

      ModelCore ClaimsKey Example
      Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM, Kroll & Stewart 1984)- Separate L1/L2 lexical stores; shared conceptual store.
      - Beginners: L2 → L1 → Concept (e.g., cat → gato → “cat”).
      - Advanced: Direct L2 → Concept links.      		Spanish L1 learners of English: Early reliance on gato to understand cat; advanced learners link cat directly to “cat.”
      BIA+ Model- Three levels: Feature (letters/sounds), Lexical (L1/L2 words), Conceptual (lemmas).
      - Activation spreads across levels (no “language nodes”); context selects the target word.      		Seeing blue activates English blue, Dutch blut (“pale”), and blus (“blouse”) — context (English reading) selects blue.

    3. Empirical Evidence for Integrated Lexicon

      • Cross-Linguistic Activation: Spanish-English bilinguals hearing “Pass the cup” look at a spoon (Spanish cuchara, starts with /k/) — proves L2 activation (Marian & Spivey 2003).
      • Homograph Effect: Dutch-English bilinguals slow down when reading ramp (English = “slope”; Dutch = “disaster”) — interference from L2 meaning (van Heuven et al. 2008).
      • Neuroimaging: High-proficiency Chinese-English bilinguals activate the same brain areas (left frontal/temporal) for L1 and L2 — no separate “language regions” (Chee et al. 1999).

    4. Factors Influencing Lexical Access

      • Proficiency: Low L2 proficiency → more brain activation (compensatory processing); high proficiency → native-like activation.
      • Frequency: High-frequency words (e.g., the) are accessed faster; “personal frequency” (how often a learner uses a word) matters more than corpus frequency.

    Key Study Example #

    • Chee et al. (2001) Proficiency Study: Low-proficiency Chinese L2 English learners activate the right inferior frontal gyrus (compensation); advanced learners activate only left hemisphere areas (same as L1) — proves proficiency shapes access efficiency.

    Section 9.2: Inhibition of One Language to Speak Another #

    Key Takeaways #

    Bilinguals use inhibition to suppress non-target languages (Green’s Model). Inhibiting the dominant L1 (to speak L2) is more effortful, causing “switch costs” — but constant inhibition strengthens executive function (bilingual cognitive advantage).

    Core Concepts & Evidence #

    1. Green’s Inhibitory Control Model (1998)

      • Core Problem: Parallel language activation (e.g., cuchara activates when speaking English spoon) requires inhibition to avoid mixing.
      • Language Task Schema: A top-down mechanism that:
        1. Identifies the target language (e.g., English) via context.
        2. Inhibits non-target language activation (e.g., Spanish).
        3. Selects the target word (e.g., spoon).
      • Dominance Effect: Inhibiting L1 (dominant) for L2 production requires more effort than inhibiting L2 for L1.

    2. Empirical Proof of Inhibition

      • Switch Costs (Meuter & Allport 1999): Dutch-English bilinguals naming numerals show larger delays when switching L2→L1 (+90ms) than L1→L2 (+50ms) — L1 inhibition “spills over” to slow L1 access.
      • ERP N2 Component: Switch trials (e.g., L1→L2) show 20% larger N2 amplitudes (neural marker of inhibition) than non-switch trials — confirms active inhibition (Abutalebi 2008).
      • Voluntary Switching (Gollan & Ferreira 2009): Even when choosing to switch, bilinguals have higher RT costs for L2→L1 — L1 inhibition is persistent.

    3. Bilingual Cognitive Advantage

      • Constant inhibition strengthens executive function:
        • Stroop Test: Bilinguals name ink color (e.g., “red” in blue) 15% faster than monolinguals.
        • Task Switching: Bilingual children switch between sorting tasks 20% faster.
      • Neural Basis: More efficient anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) — the brain’s “conflict resolver” (Abutalebi et al. 2012).

    Key Study Example #

    • Meuter & Allport (1999) Numeral Naming: Japanese-English bilinguals (Japanese-dominant) show L2→L1 switch costs of +85ms (vs. L1→L2 +45ms) — proves dominant L1 requires stronger inhibition, supporting Green’s Model.

    Section 9.3: Morphological Decomposition in the Lexicon #

    Key Takeaways #

    L2 learners decompose polymorphemic words (e.g., worked) differently than natives: Beginners store words as wholes (declarative memory), while advanced learners decompose regular inflections (procedural memory). Morphologically rich languages (e.g., Russian) force earlier decomposition.

    Core Concepts & Evidence #

    1. Ullman’s Declarative/Procedural Model
      Two memory systems drive morphological processing:

      Memory SystemFunctionRole in MorphologyExample
      DeclarativeStores arbitrary info (vocab, facts).Whole-word storage (irregular/rare words).kept (stored as a whole, not keep + -t).
      ProceduralApplies rules to combine units.Decomposition (regular/frequent words).worked = work + -ed (via past tense rule).
      • L1 vs. L2: Adults rely more on declarative memory for L2 morphology (procedural memory weakens with age).

    2. Testing Decomposition

      • Lexical Decision Task: If decomposed, stem frequency (not whole-word frequency) drives RT — natives show no frequency effect for regulars (e.g., worked vs. scribbled); L2 learners do (store whole words).
      • Masked Priming: A masked stem (e.g., work) speeds RT for worked if decomposed — natives show priming; beginners do not (Neubauer & Clahsen 2009).

    3. L2 Decomposition Patterns

      • Beginners: Store all polymorphemic words as wholes (e.g., gearbeitet [“worked” in German] = whole word, not arbeit + -t).
      • Advanced: Decompose regular inflections (e.g., -ed, -s) but struggle with derivations (e.g., bitterness from bitter).
      • Morphologically Rich Languages: Russian learners decompose inflections (e.g., rabotaju = rabotaj + -u) early — too many forms to store whole (Gor & Jackson 2013).

    Key Study Example #

    • Neubauer & Clahsen (2009) German Participles: German natives show no frequency effect for regulars (decomposed) but a frequency effect for irregulars (stored whole). Polish L2 learners show a frequency effect for both — proves they store all participles as wholes (declarative memory).

    Section 9.4: Argument Structure #

    Key Takeaways #

    Argument structure (verb meaning → required arguments, e.g., break needs Agent + Theme) is challenging for L2 learners due to cross-linguistic mismatches. Overt morphology (e.g., Spanish se for inchoatives) and universal semantic primitives (e.g., CAUSE, BECOME) help acquisition.

    Core Concepts & Evidence #

    1. Key Alternations

      • Causative-Inchoative Alternation:
        • Causative: John broke the vase (Agent + Theme).
        • Inchoative: The vase broke (Theme only).
        • Cross-Linguistic Marking: Spanish uses se (El barco se hundió = “The ship sank”); English has no marking — Spanish L1 learners master the alternation faster.
      • Dative Alternation:
        • Double-object: Mary sent John the package (entails possession).
        • Dative: Mary sent the package to John (no possession).
        • L2 Error: Overgeneralization to verbs like push (*Sam pushed Molly the package) — ignores possession constraints.

    2. Cross-Linguistic Mismatches: Psychological Verbs

      • English conflates CAUSE + STATE in one verb: The book disappointed Mary (Theme = book, Experiencer = Mary).
      • Chinese requires a periphrastic structure: Nei ben shu rang Zhang San shiwang = “That book made Zhang San disappointed.”
      • Juffs (1996): Advanced Chinese L1 learners accept The book disappointed Mary at 85% accuracy — prove they acquire L2 conflation via universal primitives.

    3. Unaccusativity: Universal Split

      • Unergatives: Agent subject (e.g., John danced — allows cognate objects: John danced a jig).
      • Unaccusatives: Theme subject (e.g., The vase broke — no cognate objects: *The vase broke a break).
      • L2 Challenge: German uses auxiliaries (sein for unaccusatives, haben for unergatives) — English L1 learners struggle to match auxiliaries to verb type.

    Key Study Example #

    • Montrul (2000) Causative-Inchoative Study: L2 Spanish learners (L1 English) use se in inchoatives at 90% accuracy by intermediate level (vs. 65% for English L2 learners of Spanish) — overt morphology provides clear argument structure cues.

    Section 9.5: Transfer of Reference #

    Key Takeaways #

    Transfer of reference (metonymy) is extending a word’s referent (e.g., ham sandwich = “customer”). L2 learners master regular metonyms (lexicalized, e.g., Bordeaux = “wine”) faster than novel metonyms (context-dependent, e.g., seat 19 = “passenger”) due to cultural gaps and processing load.

    Core Concepts & Evidence #

    1. What Is Metonymy?

      • Conceptual mapping between related entities (e.g., object→person: ham sandwich = customer; place→event: Vietnam = “Vietnam War”).
      • Distinction from Metaphor: Metonymy = association (The White House = U.S. government); metaphor = similarity (He is a lion = brave).

    2. Regular vs. Novel Metonymy

      TypeTraitsExample
      RegularLexicalized (dictionary-listed), no context needed.Read Dickens = “read books by Dickens”; Bordeaux = “wine from Bordeaux.”
      NovelContext-dependent, requires inference.Read Needham = “read books by Needham”; seat 19 = “passenger in seat 19.”

    3. L2 Acquisition Challenges

      • Cultural Gaps: The White House issued a statement — learners without U.S. context misinterpret “White House” as a building.
      • Processing Load: Novel metonyms require extra inference — Dutch-English bilinguals take 510ms to process read Needham (vs. 380ms for read Dickens, Frisson & Pickering 2007).
      • L1 Transfer: Spanish learners overuse “place for event” metonyms (mirroring Spanish) but adapt with proficiency (Slabakova et al. 2014).

    Key Study Example #

    • Frisson & Pickering (2007) Eye-Tracking: Advanced Dutch-English bilinguals have 98% accuracy for regular metonyms (read Dickens) but 72% for novel ones (read Needham) — proves context and lexicalization drive comprehension.

    3. Post-Class Review Tools #

    3.1 Chapter Takeaways Summary #

    1. Bilingual lexicon is integrated — L1/L2 words activate together; access depends on proficiency and context.
    2. Inhibiting dominant L1 causes switch costs but strengthens executive function (bilingual advantage).
    3. L2 morphological decomposition shifts from whole-word (beginners) to rule-based (advanced) — driven by memory systems.
    4. Argument structure acquisition relies on universal semantic primitives and overt morphology.
    5. Regular metonyms are easier to acquire than novel ones — cultural knowledge and lexicalization matter.

    3.2 Self-Check Questions #

    1. How do RHM and BIA+ differ in explaining L2→concept links for beginners?
    2. Why do L2→L1 switch costs (e.g., Spanish→English→Spanish) exceed L1→L2 costs?
    3. What memory system do beginners use for worked? What about advanced learners?
    4. Why do Chinese L1 learners of English initially avoid The book disappointed Mary?
    5. What makes Bordeaux (regular metonym) easier for L2 learners than seat 19 (novel metonym)?
