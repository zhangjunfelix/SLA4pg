    After-Class Research Task: Analyzing Real Second Language Morphosyntactic Data #

    A Mini Research Project on Interlanguage Morphosyntax** #

    Deadline: April 17th, 2026 #

    Task Overview #

    In this task, you will carry out a small-scale second language acquisition (SLA) research project based on real learner data. Your goal is to collect, organize, and analyze authentic second language data in order to identify morphosyntactic patterns, formulate generalizations, and develop theoretically informed hypotheses about learner knowledge and interlanguage development.

    This task requires a more explicit connection between data analysis, SLA theory, and research methodology.

    Learning Objectives #

    By completing this task, you should be able to:

    • collect and document authentic second language learner data in an ethical and systematic way
    • identify and classify morphosyntactic patterns in learner production
    • develop data-based generalizations about learner morphosyntactic knowledge
    • formulate theoretically informed hypotheses about learner grammar and development
    • connect empirical observations to concepts and debates in SLA research
    • present a concise academic-style mini analysis

    Analytical Focus: Morphosyntax #

    Your analysis must focus on second language morphosyntactic knowledge.

    Possible morphosyntactic domains #

    • tense and aspect
    • subject–verb agreement
    • finiteness
    • articles and definiteness
    • plural morphology
    • auxiliary systems
    • negation
    • interrogative formation
    • pronouns and case
    • copula and existential constructions
    • word order
    • subordination
    • infinitives and gerunds
    • relative clauses
    • argument structure patterns
    • nominal agreement
    • functional morphology more broadly

    Advice on feature selection #

    Choose a feature set that:

    • appears multiple times in your dataset
    • allows you to observe variation or stability
    • can support pattern-based analysis rather than sentence-by-sentence commentary
    • can be meaningfully linked to SLA theory

    Part 1. Collect Real Learner Data #

    Your task #

    Collect a small but analyzable dataset of real second language production.

    The learner(s) may be:

    • international students
    • classmates or colleagues who are L2 users
    • friends or family members learning a second language
    • any accessible learner of English or another target language

    Minimum data requirements #

    You must collect one of the following:

    at least 50 learner-produced sentences

    Factors to consider

    • proficiency levels
    • first language backgrounds
    • learning contexts
    • lengths of exposure to the target language

    Data source requirements #

    The data must be:

    • produced by the learner(s), not copied from textbooks, websites, apps, or AI tools
    • either spoken, written, or a combination of both
    • rich enough for morphosyntactic analysis
    • collected in a way that preserves the original learner forms

    Part 2. Recommended Data Collection Methods #

    Option A. Semi-structured interview #

    Conduct a short interview designed to elicit natural production across different time frames and structures.

    Suggested prompts:

    • Can you describe your daily routine?
    • What did you do last weekend?
    • Tell me about your language learning experience.
    • What are your plans for the future?
    • Describe a difficult or memorable experience.
    • What do you think makes a good teacher or student?
    • Have you ever learned another language before?

    Option B. Picture-based elicitation #

    Use one picture or a sequence of pictures to elicit narrative or descriptive language.

    You may target structures such as:

    • tense and aspect
    • clause linking
    • reference tracking
    • word order
    • agreement
    • nominal morphology

    Option C. Guided writing #

    Ask the learner to produce a short written text of about 200–400 words on one of the following:

    • My experience learning English
    • A challenge I faced recently
    • Differences between studying in two places
    • My future goals
    • A memorable event
    • My opinion about language learning

    Option D. Targeted elicitation #

    Design a focused task to elicit a particular morphosyntactic domain, for example:

    • tense/aspect contrasts
    • article use
    • plural marking
    • question formation
    • negation
    • relative clauses
    • agreement patterns

    If you choose this option, explain clearly why your elicitation design is appropriate for the feature you want to study.

    Part 3. Ethical Guidelines #

    Before collecting the data:

    • explain that the data will be used for a course assignment
    • obtain the learner’s permission
    • do not include the learner’s real name in the submission
    • anonymize all identifying information

    Use a pseudonym such as:

    • Learner A
    • Participant 1
    • Speaker X

    If you audio-record spoken data, make sure the learner agrees to being recorded.

    Part 4. Data Preparation and Transcription #

    Present the learner data in a clear and consistent format.

    Formatting rules #

    • number each sentence or utterance
    • preserve the learner’s original wording
    • do not silently correct non-target-like forms
    • if needed, add the intended meaning in parentheses
    • for spoken data, you may lightly clean false starts or fillers, but do not remove relevant grammatical evidence
    • if you omit part of a longer utterance, use [...]

    Example #

    1. He go to school every day.
    2. Yesterday I am very tired because I finish assignment late.
    3. She doesn’t likes coffee.
    4. I have seen him last week.
    5. There is many students in the classroom.

    Part 5. Required Analytical Steps #

    Step 1. Describe the dataset #

    Briefly describe:

    • who the learner is in general terms
    • the learner’s L1 background if relevant
    • the target language
    • the collection method
    • the amount and type of data
    • any relevant contextual factors such as length of study or learning environment

    Step 2. Identify recurring patterns #

    Look for repeated morphosyntactic behaviors.

    Ask questions such as:

    • Which forms are used accurately and consistently?
    • Which forms are variable?
    • Which forms are omitted, overgeneralized, or used in non-target-like ways?
    • Do the same patterns recur across contexts?
    • Is there evidence of partial rule knowledge?

    Step 3. Group and code the data #

    Organize the examples into categories.

    Possible categories include:

    • target-like use
    • omission
    • overgeneralization
    • substitution
    • non-target word order
    • variability across similar contexts

    You may present your dataset in a small coding table if useful.

    Step 4. Generalize from the data #

    Write 3–5 generalizations about the learner’s interlanguage grammar.

    Your generalizations should be pattern-based, not anecdotal.

    Step 5. Develop hypotheses #

    Propose 2–4 theoretically informed hypotheses.

    Your hypotheses may draw on concepts such as:

    • interlanguage development
    • transfer from the first language
    • developmental constraints
    • underspecification of functional morphology
    • limited morphological realization despite syntactic knowledge
    • input frequency and salience
    • variability and processing pressure
    • form–function mismatch
    • feature reassembly
    • interface-related difficulty

    Step 6. Connect to SLA literature #

    Briefly relate your findings to at least 2 academic sources from SLA literature.

    You may use the literature to:

    • support an interpretation
    • compare your learner with known developmental tendencies
    • highlight a debate
    • explain why the pattern matters theoretically

    You do not need a full literature review, but you should show that your analysis is informed by SLA research.

    Part 6. Suggested Guiding Questions #

    You may use the following questions to guide your analysis:

    1. What does the learner appear to know about the target structure?
    2. What remains unstable or inconsistent?
    3. Are the non-target-like forms random, or do they reflect a pattern?
    4. Is there evidence of systematic variability?
    5. Could the pattern reflect transfer, developmental sequence, processing limitations, or input effects?
    6. Does the learner show stronger control of lexical meaning than grammatical morphology?
    7. What does this dataset suggest about the learner’s current interlanguage system?

    Part 7. Suggested Report Structure #

    1. Introduction #

    Briefly state the purpose of the task and identify the morphosyntactic feature(s) you will analyze.

    2. Learner Background and Data Collection #

    Provide a short description of the learner(s), context, and data collection method.

    3. Dataset #

    Present the learner data clearly.

    4. Analytical Focus and Method #

    State which morphosyntactic feature(s) you selected and explain how you analyzed the data.

    5. Findings #

    Describe the main patterns with supporting examples.

    6. Generalizations and Hypotheses #

    Present your broader conclusions about the learner’s interlanguage knowledge and your hypotheses about why these patterns appear.

    7. Connection to SLA Literature #

    Relate your findings to at least two relevant academic sources.

    8. Reflection #

    Comment briefly on the methodological and theoretical value of working with real learner data.

    Part 8. Length Requirement #

    Suggested length #

    • 1000 words
    • excluding references and appendices

    Part 9. Submission Format #

    Naming format for the submission #

    Name your sumbission as: “Name-QS1”

    You may submit as:

    • a Word document
    • a PDF

    Part 10. Assessment Criteria #

    Your work will be evaluated based on the following:

    • quality and relevance of the learner data
    • clarity of morphosyntactic focus
    • accuracy and depth of analysis
    • strength of pattern-based generalizations
    • quality of theoretical hypotheses
    • ability to connect data to SLA literature
    • organization, clarity, and academic presentation

    Optional Template for Students #

    Title #

    A Mini Analysis of Interlanguage Morphosyntax in Learner A

    1. Introduction #

    State the goal of the analysis and the target feature(s).

    2. Learner Background and Data Collection #

    Introduce the learner and explain how the data was collected.

    3. Dataset #

    Present the learner sentences or utterances.

    4. Analytical Focus #

    Explain which morphosyntactic feature(s) you selected and why.

    5. Findings #

    Describe the main patterns and illustrate them with examples.

    6. Generalizations #

    Summarize the broader regularities in the learner’s language.

    7. Hypotheses #

    Propose theoretically informed explanations.

    8. Connection to SLA Literature #

    Relate your observations to relevant research.

    9. Reflection #

    Explain what this task reveals about learner language and SLA research methods.

    Note #

    This task is not simply an error-hunting exercise. Your goal is to treat learner language as structured evidence of an evolving interlanguage system. A strong submission will move beyond surface correction and will instead show how real learner data can be used to make principled claims about morphosyntactic knowledge, variability, and second language development.
