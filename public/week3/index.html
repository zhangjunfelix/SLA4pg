    Week 3 — Processing & the Parser: How L2 is Used in Real Time #

    Anchor text: Slabakova (2016), Second Language Acquisition, Chapter 3.

    ⭐ Overview (for preview) #

    • How the mind parses language incrementally and what this implies for second language (L2) comprehension and production.
    • Core processing architectures and why performance sometimes diverges from grammatical competence.
    • What L2 processing predicts for error signatures, reaction times, and eye-movement patterns, and how methods can detect them.

    🎯 Learning goals #

    By the end of this week you should be able to:

    1. Explain how incremental parsing builds structure on the fly and where garden-paths come from.
    2. Distinguish competence vs. performance explanations for L2 behavior.
    3. Describe key processing models (e.g., cue-based retrieval; good-enough processing) and their L2 predictions.
    4. Propose a testable design (e.g., SPR, eye-tracking, ERP) linking a processing claim to measurable outcomes.

    📖 Reading (do before Session 1) #

    • Required: Slabakova (2016), Chapter 3 (focus on: processing architectures; ambiguity resolution; memory limitations; predictions for L2).
    • Optional: Skim the methodological overviews in Ch. 12 to preview tools we’ll use later (GJT, SPR, eye-tracking, ERP).

    Guiding questions:

    • Why does incremental interpretation sometimes lead to misanalysis?
    • What would count as evidence that a difficulty is processing-based rather than representational?

    🧠 Key terms #

    incremental parsing; garden-path; ambiguity resolution; reanalysis; cue-based retrieval; similarity-based interference; working memory; local vs. structural distance; good-enough processing; surprisal; speed–accuracy trade-off; offline vs. online measures

    🔍 Core ideas #

    • Incrementality: The parser commits early to a structure based on probabilistic cues; strong priors can mislead (garden-paths) and require reanalysis.
    • Competence vs. performance: A learner may know a structure (competence) yet still read it more slowly or err under time pressure (performance).
    • Cue-based retrieval: Integration depends on retrieving the correct dependency from memory; interference occurs when distractors share features (e.g., number).
    • Good-enough processing: Under load, the system may settle for plausible representations without full detail, increasing shallow-structure outcomes.
    • Measurement matters: Online methods (SPR, eye-tracking, ERP) detect time-course effects that offline accuracy may miss.

    🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1) #

    1) What does the parser do, and when? #

    • Key notes: Parsing is incremental—word-by-word commitments; ambiguity is resolved using lexical, syntactic, and discourse cues. Early commitments can create garden-paths (e.g., “While Anna dressed the baby…”) requiring reanalysis.
    • Class activity idea: Read classic garden-path sentences aloud; predict where reanalysis will occur and why.

    2) Sources of difficulty: ambiguity & memory #

    • Key notes: Ambiguities (attachment, filler–gap, relative clauses) and memory constraints drive slowdowns. Long dependencies and structural complexity increase retrieval demands.
    • Example: Subject vs. object relative clauses; the object relatives often incur larger integration costs, especially for L2 readers.

    3) Cue-based retrieval & interference #

    • Key notes: The system retrieves earlier material by matching features (e.g., number, gender). Similarity-based interference (distractors share features) produces slowdowns or attraction errors (e.g., “The key to the cabinets are…”).
    • Discussion: Predict which L1→L2 pairings heighten interference for agreement dependencies.

    4) Competence vs. performance: L2 implications #

    • Key notes: Slower or less accurate online performance does not imply lack of competence. Processing load, not representational deficit, can explain delayed or shallow effects—especially under time pressure.
    • Activity: For a phenomenon you know (e.g., verb movement or agreement), sketch both a competence account and a processing account. What data would differentiate them?

    🧪 Methods micro-lab (Session 2) #

    Goal: Link a processing claim to an empirical signature.

    Choose one design and complete the template.

    • Self-Paced Reading (SPR)

      • Claim: L2 readers experience interference in long-distance agreement.
      • Prediction: Longer reading times at the agreement region when a distractor noun mismatches the subject’s number.
      • Measure: Region-by-region RTs; compare match vs. mismatch conditions.

    • Eye-tracking during Reading

      • Claim: L2 readers show extended regressions in garden-paths.
      • Prediction: Increased first-pass and regression-path times at the disambiguating region.
      • Measure: First fixation, gaze duration, regression path, total time.

    • ERP

      • Claim: L2 detects morpho-syntactic violations but with delayed/attenuated responses.
      • Prediction: P600 modulation for agreement violations; latency/amplitude differences vs. L1.
      • Measure: Component amplitude/latency windows; artifact rejection; item-level averaging.

    🧩 In-class application (Session 3) #

    Case vignette: Agreement attraction in L2 English

    • Sentence frame: “The key to the cabinets are/is on the table.”
      Questions:
    • What interference pattern do you predict for L1s with rich agreement vs. poor agreement systems?
    • If offline accuracy is native-like but online times show large slowdowns, what does that imply?
    • Propose one instructional activity that targets agreement dependencies under processing load.

    ✅ Self-check (answers hidden) #

    1. Why do garden-path sentences cause slowdowns?
    1. Define similarity-based interference in cue-based retrieval.
    1. Give one reason online measures can diverge from offline accuracy in L2.
    1. What pattern would support a processing (not competence) account for an L2 difficulty?
    1. Name one ERP component associated with morpho-syntactic violations and its interpretation.

    📝 Weekly analytical note (due before Week 4) #

    In ~300 words, pick one phenomenon (garden-paths, agreement attraction, or relative clause attachment). (a) State a processing-based claim, (b) derive a time-course prediction (which region/measure), and (c) choose a method (SPR, eye-tracking, or ERP) with one analytic decision you would preregister.
