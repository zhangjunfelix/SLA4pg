    Week 8 — The Mental Lexicon: Representation, Access, and Argument Structure #

    Anchor text: Slabakova (2016), Second Language Acquisition, Chapter 9.

    ⭐ Overview (for preview) #

    • How the bilingual mental lexicon is organized and accessed in real time: cross-language activation, inhibition, cognate/false-friend effects.
    • What L2 learners must acquire beyond word form and core meaning: feature bundles, morphological decomposition, collocations, and argument-structure mappings.
    • How to test lexical representation and access with lexical decision, (masked) priming, picture naming, and eye-tracking/ERP.

    🎯 Learning goals #

    By the end of this week you should be able to:

    1. Describe major models of the bilingual lexicon and predict patterns for cognates, interlingual homographs, and translation equivalents.
    2. Explain cross-language activation and inhibition and how they show up in RTs, accuracy, and priming.
    3. Distinguish form, lemma-level (syntactic/semantic), and morphological representations; predict when decomposition facilitates or hinders L2 processing.
    4. Explain argument-structure and subcategorization learning (e.g., which verbs take which complements) and design a compact test of lexical–syntactic mapping.

    📖 Reading (do before Session 1) #

    • Required: Slabakova (2016), Ch. 9 (focus on: bilingual lexicon models, lexical access/inhibition, cognates & homographs, morphological decomposition, argument structure).
    • Optional: Revisit Ch. 3 (processing) for methods links and time-course interpretations.

    Guiding questions:

    • Why do cognates often help but interlingual homographs can hurt performance?
    • How do learners acquire which complements a verb licenses (e.g., explain something to someone vs. discuss about)?

    🧠 Key terms #

    bilingual lexicon; cross-language activation; inhibition; cognate; interlingual homograph; translation asymmetry; lemma; subcategorization frame; argument structure; morphological decomposition; derivational vs. inflectional morphology; lexical decision; masked priming; picture naming; N400; frequency/AoA effects; collocation.

    🔍 Core ideas #

    • Nonselective access: Both languages are co-activated; the L2 user must inhibit the non-target language—predicts cognate facilitation and homograph interference.
    • Levels of representation:
      • Form (orthography/phonology)
      • Lemma (syntactic/semantic info; subcategorization, argument structure)
      • Morphology (decomposition: stems + affixes; feature bundles).
    • Morphology & the lexicon: L2 readers may decompose reliably for frequent/transparent affixes but show costs for opaque or low-frequency forms.
    • Argument structure learning: Beyond meanings of words, learners must map who does what to whom (frames and complements). Negative transfer yields errors like discuss about or misusing causative/inchoative alternations.
    • Methods matter: Lexical decision and masked priming capture early form/lemma activation; picture naming taps production; N400 modulations and eye-tracking show time-course of lexical–semantic integration.

    🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1, 45 min) #

    1) Models & predictions of the bilingual lexicon #

    • Key notes: Lexical access is largely nonselective; L1 and L2 compete/cooperate. Expect cognate speed-ups, homograph slowdowns, and translation asymmetries (L2→L1 often easier than L1→L2 early on).
    • Class spark: Elicit quick examples of cognates and false friends from students’ languages; predict RT/accuracy effects.

    2) Representation levels & morphological decomposition #

    • Key notes: Separate form knowledge from lemma (subcategorization, semantic selection) and morphology (stems/affixes). Transparency and frequency modulate decomposition; derivational morphology may be harder than inflectional in L2 reading.
    • Micro-demo: Show pairs like teacher–teach, happiness–happy, government–govern; ask which are likely decomposed online by L2 readers and why.

    3) Argument structure & subcategorization #

    • Key notes: Learners must acquire verb-specific frames (e.g., depend on X, remind NP of NP), event-structure mappings (causative/inchoative), and prepositional selection. Transfer predicts specific error signatures.
    • Think–pair–share: For three verbs (explain, discuss, provide), list the correct frames and the most likely L1-based errors.

    Pacing: ~12 min per mini-topic + ~9 min discussion fits the 45-min block.

    🧪 Methods micro-lab (Session 2, 45 min) #

    Goal: Connect a theoretical claim to a diagnostic task and measure.

    Choose one focus and complete the template.

    • A. Cognates vs. homographs (nonselective access)

      • Claim: Cross-language activation boosts cognates but harms homographs.
      • Task: Masked priming lexical decision (prime: L1 or L2; target: cognate/homograph/noncognate).
      • Prediction: Faster RTs for cognates; slower or less accurate for homographs; priming across languages.

    • B. Morphological decomposition

      • Claim: Transparent affixes support decomposition; opaque/low-frequency forms reduce it.
      • Task: Lexical decision with morphological primes (stem/affix) vs. orthographic controls.
      • Prediction: Larger priming for transparent derivations/inflections; reduced/absent priming for opaque pairs.

    • C. Argument structure frames

      • Claim: L2 learners undergeneralize/overgeneralize verb frames until frames are learned.
      • Task: Acceptability judgment on subcategorization (e.g., discuss about, explain to, provide with), plus SPR for processing cost.
      • Prediction: Offline acceptance improves with proficiency; online costs linger at preposition/complement region.

    Deliverable: 3-line prereg—hypothesis, primary DV (RT, accuracy, N400 amplitude), and falsifier.

    🧩 In-class application (Session 3, 45 min) #

    Case vignette

    • Group A (Spanish L1 → English): strong cognate facilitation; occasional false-friend errors (actual vs. actualmente).
    • Group B (Chinese L1 → English): slower derivational morphology; difficulty with argument-structure for certain verbs (provide X with Y).

    Questions:

    1. Which patterns reflect nonselective access vs. representation gaps at the lemma or morphology level?
    2. What design tweak would isolate inhibition costs (e.g., language-switching blocks)?
    3. Propose one teaching intervention per group (e.g., collocation/phrase-frame drills; contrastive lists of false friends with usage contexts).

    1. How would you detect inhibition in bilingual access?
    1. What pattern would indicate a lemma-level (not just form-level) learning effect?
