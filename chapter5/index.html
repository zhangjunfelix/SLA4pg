    Chapter 5: First Language Acquisition & Two First Languages #

    Anchor text: Slabakova, R. (2016). Second Language Acquisition. Chapter 5.
    Link to Chapter 5

    1. Pre-Class Overview & Core Orientation #

    1.1 Chapter Focus #

    This chapter explores the developmental trajectory of monolingual first language (L1) acquisition (sound, vocabulary, grammar, semantics/pragmatics) and bilingual first language (2L1) acquisition, with a focus on how children build linguistic systems from infancy to early childhood.

    1.2 Key Learning Objectives #

    By the end of this chapter, learners will be able to:

    1. Explain the stages and mechanisms of L1 sound system acquisition (perception, segmentation, production).
    2. Identify the cognitive assumptions that solve the “Gavagai Problem” in vocabulary learning.
    3. Describe the development of functional morphology (e.g., past tense) and syntax (e.g., wh-questions) in L1.
    4. Analyze why semantic (aspect) and pragmatic (scalar implicatures) acquisition are delayed relative to other linguistic domains.
    5. Distinguish between “one system” vs. “two systems” in 2L1 acquisition and recognize cross-linguistic influence.

    1.3 Guiding Questions for Reading #

    • How do infants transition from universal sound sensitivity to native phoneme categorization?
    • Why are verbs harder to learn than nouns in L1 vocabulary development?
    • What evidence supports the “two separate grammars” claim in bilingual first language acquisition?
    • How do computational complexity and cognitive maturity affect semantic/pragmatic learning?

    2.1 Section 5.1: Acquisition of the Sounds of One’s Native Language #

    Focuses on how children “break into” the speech signal by mastering three components of the sound system:

    1. Segmental Inventory: From Universal Sensitivity to Native Categorization #

    • Key Problem: Children must identify 40–50 native phonemes from 800+ global phonetic units (the “phoneme categorization problem”).
    • Developmental Stages:
      • 6–8 Months: Universal Perceptual Sensitivity
        Infants detect all linguistically relevant sound contrasts (not just native ones).
        • Example 1 (Eimas et al. 1971): 1–4 month-olds dishabituate to English /b/ vs. /p/ (e.g., bin vs. pin), proving they distinguish non-produced contrasts.
        • Example 2 (Werker & Tees 1984): 6–8 month-olds (English/Hindi-learning) distinguish Hindi dental [d] vs. retroflex [d]; by 10–12 months, only Hindi learners retain this sensitivity.
      • 10–12 Months+: Native Phoneme Categorization
        Infants “prune” universal sensitivity to focus on native phonemes (e.g., English infants retain [l]/[r] distinction; Japanese infants lose it, as Japanese merges [l]/[r] into one phoneme).

    2. Segmentation Problem: Finding Word Boundaries #

    • Key Challenge: Speech has no “silent spaces” (unlike writing); children use three language-specific cues:
      • Cue 1: Isolated Words: Caretaker speech (6–60 isolated words/hour) acts as “anchors” (e.g., knowing Daddy helps segment Daddy knows into Daddy + knows).
      • Cue 2: Prosody (Stress): The “Prosodic Bootstrapping Hypothesis”—English uses trochaic stress (first syllable stressed: BA-by, SPON-sor), so children assume stressed syllables mark word starts (90% accurate for English).
      • Cue 3: Phonotactics: Language-specific rules for sound combinations (e.g., English forbids [tl] at syllable onset, so fatlump is segmented as fat lump; Bulgarian allows [tl], so Bulgarian infants do not use [tl] as a boundary cue).

    3. Sound Production: From Approximation to Mastery #

    • Perception-Production Gap: Children understand words they cannot pronounce (e.g., clock → [gak], desk → [deks])—their phonological representations (perception) are adult-like, but articulatory skills (production) are not.
    • Typical Production Errors: Follow predictable patterns to simplify articulation:
      • Substitution: clock → [gak] (voiceless /k/ → voiced /g/).
      • Metathesis: desk → [deks] (sound reordering: /dɛsk/ → /dɛks/).
      • Consonant Harmony: dog → [gog] (initial consonant matches final one).

    2.2 Section 5.2: Learning Word Meanings #

    Explores the efficiency of L1 vocabulary growth and the cognitive tools that resolve meaning ambiguity.

    1. Vocabulary Growth: Timeline & Efficiency #

    • Developmental Milestones:
      • 12 months: 1st words (object labels/social terms: Daddy, juice).
      • 18–20 months: 50 words (slow growth, as children test meaning hypotheses).
      • 20+ months: “Great vocabulary spurt” (1–2 new words/day).
      • Ages 2–6: 10 words/day (add abstract terms: happy, yesterday).
    • Proficiency Benchmarks: Age 6 = 14,000 words; High school = 60,000 words (including academic vocabulary).
    • Key Observation: L1 learning is far more efficient than L2 (2-year-olds learn 70+ words/week; adults learn 5–10 L2 words/week).

    2. The Gavagai Problem & Cognitive Assumptions #

    • Key Problem (Quine 1960): When hearing gavagai while seeing a rabbit, children cannot logically know if it means “rabbit,” “furry,” or “running”—they use 4 innate assumptions to narrow meaning:
      • Whole Object Assumption (Markman & Hutchison 1984): New words label entire objects (e.g., gavagai = whole rabbit, not its paw).
      • Type Assumption (Clark 1993): New words label categories (e.g., dog = all dogs, not just “Fido”).
      • Basic Level Assumption (Hall & Waxman 1993): New words refer to mid-level categories (e.g., dog instead of superordinate mammal or subordinate golden retriever).
      • Mutual Exclusivity Assumption (Markman & Wachtel 1988): No two words have identical meanings (e.g., if a child knows spoon, they infer fendle refers to an unfamiliar whisk).

    3. Verb Meaning Acquisition: Challenges & Syntactic Bootstrapping #

    • Why Verbs Are Harder: They require linking abstract actions to thematic roles (e.g., eat needs an Agent = eater, Theme = food) and depend on context.
    • Syntactic Bootstrapping (Gleitman 1990): Children use a verb’s syntactic frame to infer meaning (e.g., Mary clessed the toy car to her baby brother → frame = Noun-Verb-Noun-to-Noun → meaning = “transfer” (like give)).
    • Verb Overgeneralization: Normal developmental step (e.g., Don’t giggle me—extending intransitive giggle to transitive frame; I’m singing him—extending sing to “sing to him”).

    2.3 Section 5.3: Acquisition of Functional Morphology and Syntax #

    Focuses on how children learn grammatical rules (functional morphology) and sentence structure (syntax).

    1. Functional Morphology: Rules, Errors, and Developmental Order #

    • Functional vs. Lexical Morphology: Functional morphemes (e.g., -ing, -s plural) are grammatical “engine” components; lexical morphemes (e.g., mommy, run) carry meaning.
    • Rule Learning: The Wug Test (Berko Gleason 1958):
      • Design: 4–5 year-olds saw a “wug” (novel creature); when shown two, they were asked “Now there are two __?”
      • Finding: 75% produced wugs (plural) and wugged (past tense)—proving children learn general rules, not just memorize words.
    • U-Shaped Development (Past Tense):
      • Stage 1 (Early): Correct irregulars (went, ate) (memorized).
      • Stage 2 (Middle): Overgeneralization (goed, eated) (applying -ed rule to all verbs).
      • Stage 3 (Late): Correct regulars (walked) and irregulars (went) (restricting rules to regular verbs).
    • Brown’s 14 Grammatical Morphemes (Brown 1973): Universal acquisition order (tied to complexity and phonetic salience):
      1. Progressive -ing (19–28 months)
      2. Prepositions in/on (27–33 months)
      3. Regular plural -s (27–33 months)
      4. Irregular past (25–46 months)
      5. Regular past -ed (26–48 months)
      6. 3SG -s (28–50 months)

    2. Syntax Acquisition: Stages, MLU, and Complex Structures #

    • Developmental Stages:
      1. One-Word Stage (12–18 months): Single words stand for full propositions (e.g., cow = “Look at the cow”).
      2. Two-Word Stage (18–24 months): Follows native word order (e.g., English SVO: Mommy fix, give doggie)—reflects emerging thematic role knowledge (Agent, Theme).
      3. Multiple-Word Stage (24+ months): Longer utterances with functional morphemes (e.g., I’m gonna sit on her) and complex structures (relative clauses, questions).
    • MLU (Mean Length of Utterance): Measures syntactic growth (total morphemes in 100 utterances ÷ 100). Example: Eve (20 months) = MLU 1.7 (mommy go); Eve (28 months) = MLU 3.5 (mommy is driving the car).
    • Complex Structures: Wh-Question Asymmetry:
      • Subject Questions (e.g., Who is helping Mary?): Simple (no auxiliary movement over subject)—high accuracy.
      • Object Questions (e.g., Who is Mary helping?): Complex (wh-word moves from object to start, crossing more syntactic nodes)—low accuracy (children simplify: What did he play? instead of What did he play with?).

    2.4 Section 5.4: Acquisition of Semantics and Pragmatics #

    Explores the acquisition of abstract meaning (semantics) and context-dependent communication (pragmatics), both delayed due to computational complexity.

    1. Semantic Acquisition: Viewpoint Aspect #

    • Viewpoint Aspect: Encodes how speakers “view” an event (imperfective = ongoing; perfective = completed).
    • Cross-Linguistic Errors:
      • Example 1 (Italian): 3–5 year-olds accept completed events for imperfect tense (e.g., Marta mangiava una mela = “Marta was eating an apple” → accept “Marta ate the whole apple”); adults reject this.
      • Example 2 (Russian): 3–6 year-olds guess randomly at imperfective verbs without temporal context; with context (While he was writing, the phone rang), accuracy jumps to 80%.
    • Why Aspect Is Hard: Requires linking morphology to abstract event properties (not concrete objects) and integrating discourse context (mastered by age 6+).

    2. Pragmatic Acquisition: Scalar Implicatures #

    • Scalar Implicatures: Inferences where a weaker term (e.g., some) implies negation of a stronger term (e.g., not all).
    • Study Example (Papafragou & Musolino 2003):
      • Design: All 5 horses jump; puppet says “Some of the horses jumped.”
      • Findings: Adults (92.5%) reject the description (some = not all); 5-year-olds (12.5%) accept it (interpret some literally = “at least one”).
    • Why Implicatures Are Hard: Children default to literal meaning—they lack perspective-taking skills to ask “Why didn’t the puppet say all?”
    • Improvement Strategies: Explicit scales (some vs. all) or context highlighting speaker intent boost 6-year-olds’ accuracy to 70%.

    3. Unifying Constraint: Computational Complexity #

    • Key Claim: Semantic/pragmatic delays stem from needing to compare multiple meaning options and integrate context—taxing working memory.
      • Semantics (Aspect): Compare “incomplete” vs. “completed” event views.
      • Pragmatics (Implicatures): Compare literal (some = at least one) vs. pragmatic (some = not all) meanings and infer speaker intent.
    • Developmental Timeline: Improves with working memory/cognitive maturity (ages 6–8).

    2.5 Section 5.5: Bilingual First Language Acquisition #

    Resolves the “one system vs. two systems” debate and explains cross-linguistic influence in 2L1 learners.

    1. Evidence for Two Differentiated Grammatical Systems #

    • Early Language Separation: 8–12 month-old bilinguals show language-specific sensitivity (e.g., distinguish L1/L2 prosody; use L1 with L1-only caregivers, L2 with L2-only caregivers—no code-switching in monolingual interactions).
    • Matching Monolingual Stages (Genesee 1989, Meisel 1989): French-English/German-French bilinguals reach the two-word stage (18–24 months) and acquire morphology (e.g., English -ing, French -ant) at the same age as monolinguals.
    • Monolingual-Like Errors: Spanish-English bilinguals make Spanish null-subject errors (Hablas español = “Speak Spanish”)—same as monolingual Spanish children; no mixed errors (Speak español).

    2. Cross-Linguistic Influence: Constraints and Examples #

    Influence occurs in specific domains, not randomly:

    • Domain 1: Syntax-Discourse Interface:
      • Example (Pronoun Reference): English-Italian bilinguals make 30% more errors in Italian (interpreting null pronoun ø as referring to the daughter, not mother)—English’s ambiguous pronouns “bleed” into Italian.
      • Example (Null-Subject Transfer): Spanish-English bilinguals use 25% more overt subjects in Spanish (Tú hablas español) than monolinguals—transferring English’s requirement for overt subjects.
    • Domain 2: Processing Economy:
      • Example (Dutch-French Wh-Questions): French allows wh-in-situ (Tu fais quoi? = “What are you doing?”); Dutch requires wh-movement (Wat doe je?). Bilinguals produce 40% wh-in-situ errors in Dutch (Jij doe wat?)—transferring French’s simpler structure to reduce processing load.

    3. Factors Shaping Cross-Linguistic Influence #

    Three key determinants:

    • 1. Input Quantity (Language Dominance): Influence flows from dominant (more exposure) to non-dominant language. Example: L2-dominant (80% English) German-English bilinguals make 60% German gender errors (die Hund instead of der Hund)—transferring English’s lack of grammatical gender.
    • 2. Structural Ambiguity: Influence occurs when one language has ambiguous structures. Example: Spanish’s Hablas español (ambiguous: “You speak” vs. “Speak”)—bilinguals interpret it as imperative 35% more often than monolinguals (reinforced by English’s single imperative analysis of Speak Spanish).
    • 3. Processing Demands: Bilinguals avoid complex structures. Example: Dutch’s wh-movement is more complex than French’s in-situ—bilinguals transfer French’s structure to Dutch.

    3. Post-Learning Consolidation #

    3.1 Chapter Takeaways #

    1. L1 acquisition follows universal stages but uses language-specific cues (e.g., English trochaic stress vs. Spanish prosody).
    2. Cognitive assumptions (whole object, mutual exclusivity) drive efficient L1 vocabulary learning.
    3. Functional morphology and syntax develop via rule-learning (not memorization), with U-shaped patterns for irregulars.
    4. Semantics/pragmatics are delayed due to computational complexity—mastered by ages 6–8.
    5. Bilingual first language learners develop two separate grammars, with cross-linguistic influence shaped by input, ambiguity, and processing demands.

    3.2 After-Class Tasks #

    1. Mini Reflection (150–200 words):
      Choose one L1 domain (sound, vocabulary, grammar) and explain how a child’s cognitive development supports learning (e.g., how working memory affects aspect acquisition).
    2. Self-Check Questions:
      1. What is the “Perception-Production Gap” in L1 sound acquisition?
      2. Why do children overgeneralize past tense rules (e.g., goed)?
      3. What evidence proves bilingual first language learners have two separate grammars?
      4. How does input quantity shape cross-linguistic influence?
