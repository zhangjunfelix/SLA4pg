## Opening Overview: What Is the Syntax–Semantics Interface?

Before we discuss the three presentation studies, we need to understand one key idea: the **syntax–semantics interface**.

This sounds difficult, but the basic idea is simple.

> **Syntax** = how words are arranged in a sentence.  
> **Semantics** = what meaning the sentence has.  
> **Syntax–semantics interface** = how sentence form creates sentence meaning.

In second language acquisition, many problems are not just about learning new words. Learners also need to learn how a language connects **forms** with **meanings**.

For example, learners may already understand meanings such as:

- a completed action
- an ongoing action
- a repeated action
- a generic statement
- a question
- a definite or indefinite referent

But different languages may express these meanings in different grammatical ways.

So the main question of this chapter is:

> How do L2 learners learn the meaning consequences of grammatical forms?

---

## 1. What Does “Interface” Mean?

An **interface** is a connection point between two parts of language.

In this chapter, the two parts are **syntax** and **semantics**.

| Part of Language | Main Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Syntax** | What is the grammatical form? | word order, tense, aspect, articles, wh-movement |
| **Semantics** | What meaning does the form create? | completion, genericity, scope, definiteness, time interpretation |

The syntax–semantics interface studies cases where a small grammatical difference leads to a difference in meaning.

---

## 2. A Simple Example

Compare these two sentences:

1. *I saw Mary cross the street.*
2. *I saw Mary crossing the street.*

They look very similar, but they do not mean exactly the same thing.

| Sentence | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *I saw Mary cross the street.* | bare verb: *cross* | Mary completed the crossing. |
| *I saw Mary crossing the street.* | progressive form: *crossing* | Mary was in the process of crossing. She may or may not have finished. |

This is a syntax–semantics interface issue.

The difference is not just vocabulary. Both sentences use almost the same words. The important difference is the grammatical form:

- *cross*
- *crossing*

This form difference changes the interpretation of the event.

---

## 3. Why Is This Important for L2 Learners?

L2 learners often need to learn new **form–meaning mappings**.

A **form–meaning mapping** means that a particular grammatical form is connected to a particular meaning.

For example:

| Meaning | Possible Grammatical Encoding |
|---|---|
| completed action | tense, aspect, verb form |
| ongoing action | progressive form |
| generic meaning | bare plural, definite plural, articles |
| scope meaning | word order, quantifier position |
| definiteness | articles, demonstratives, context |
| question meaning | wh-movement, wh-in-situ, discontinuous wh-phrases |

The challenge is that the learner’s L1 may encode the same meaning differently.

For example, two languages may both express the idea of a completed event, but they may use different grammatical forms to do so.

This means that L2 learning is not just about learning words. It is also about learning how the L2 grammar organizes meaning.

---

## 4. Why Is the Syntax–Semantics Interface Difficult?

Interface problems are difficult because learners may know the words but still misunderstand the meaning of the structure.

For example, a learner may know the English forms:

- *ate*
- *was eating*
- *eat*
- *eating*

But this is not enough. The learner must also know what each form means in different sentence structures.

| Form | Possible Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Matt ate a cake.* | Matt finished eating the cake. |
| *Matt was eating a cake.* | Matt was in the process of eating; he may or may not have finished. |
| *I saw Matt eat a cake.* | Matt finished eating the cake. |
| *I saw Matt eating a cake.* | Matt was eating the cake; completion is not required. |

So the difficulty is not only about grammar form. It is about connecting grammar form with interpretation.

---

## 5. Learning What a Sentence Cannot Mean

One important issue in this chapter is that learners must learn not only what a sentence **can** mean, but also what it **cannot** mean.

For example:

| Sentence Type | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence A | ✓ possible | ✓ possible |
| Sentence B | ✓ possible | ✗ not possible |

The difficult question is:

> How does the learner know that Meaning 2 is not possible for Sentence B?

This is related to the **poverty of the stimulus** problem.

Learners usually hear examples of what is possible. But they are not usually told directly:

> “This sentence cannot mean that.”

So if advanced L2 learners know that a certain meaning is impossible, this is very interesting. It suggests that learners may be using deeper grammatical knowledge, not just memorizing sentences from input.

## 6. The Three Presentation Studies

This chapter will be discussed through three presentation studies. Each study examines a different syntax–semantics interface problem.

---

### 6.1 Grammatical Aspect: Slabakova (2003)

This study examines how L2 learners understand English aspectual meanings.

The key contrast is:

| English Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *I saw Matt eat a cake.* | Matt finished eating the cake. |
| *I saw Matt eating a cake.* | Matt was eating; he may or may not have finished. |

The research question is:

> Can Bulgarian L1 learners of English acquire the English connection between verb form and completed-event meaning?

This is important because the meaning is subtle. Learners may not be explicitly taught that *eat a cake* in this structure implies completion.

---

### 6.2 Poverty of the Stimulus and Quantifier Scope: Marsden (2009)

Marsden’s study investigates how L2 learners understand **quantifier scope** in Japanese.

Quantifier scope concerns how words like *someone* and *every* interact in meaning.

For example:

> *Someone read every book.*

This sentence can have two possible meanings in English:

1. One person read all the books.
2. For each book, possibly a different person read it.

Another example:

> *Someone fed every cat.*

Picture A may show one person feeding all the cats.  
Picture B may show different people each feeding one different cat.

In some languages, both interpretations may be possible. In other languages, sentence structure may block one interpretation.

The research question is:

> Can L2 learners acquire that a certain interpretation is not allowed in Japanese, even if no one directly teaches them that it is impossible?

This is a poverty-of-the-stimulus issue because learners must learn the limits of interpretation.

---

### 6.3 Novel Constructions: Dekydtspotter and Sprouse (2001)

This study examines French **discontinuous interrogatives**.

French allows a type of wh-question that English does not have.

| Type | French Example | Rough English Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | *Qui de célèbre fumait...?* | Which famous person smoked...? |
| Discontinuous | *Qui fumait de célèbre...?* | Which famous person smoked...? |

The two questions look similar, but they have different interpretations.

The important question is:

> Can English L1 learners of French acquire both the new question form and its special meaning?

This is interesting because English does not have the same construction. Learners cannot simply transfer it from English.

---

## 7. How Do Researchers Study These Issues?

Researchers cannot simply ask learners:

> “Do you know the syntax–semantics interface?”

Instead, they design tasks that test how learners interpret sentences.

---

### 7.1 Truth Value Judgment Tasks

In a **truth value judgment task**, learners read or hear a context. Then they decide whether a sentence is true or false in that context.

Example:

| Context | Test Sentence | Expected Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Matt ate only half of the cake. | *I saw Matt eat a cake.* | False |
| Matt ate only half of the cake. | *I saw Matt eating a cake.* | True |

This task tests whether learners know that *eat a cake* implies completion, but *eating a cake* does not.

---

### 7.2 Acceptability Judgment Tasks

In an **acceptability judgment task**, learners judge whether a sentence sounds acceptable or appropriate.

Example:

| Sentence | Question |
|---|---|
| *Tigers eat meat.* | Can this refer to tigers in general? |
| *The tigers eat meat.* | Does this refer to tigers in general, or to a specific group of tigers? |

This kind of task helps researchers see whether learners understand the grammar and meaning restrictions of the L2.

---

### 7.3 Picture-Based Interpretation Tasks

In a picture-based task, learners look at pictures and decide which picture matches a sentence.

This is useful for studying quantifier scope.

Example sentence:

> *Someone fed every cat.*

This sentence may match two different pictures:

- Picture A: one person fed all the cats.
- Picture B: different people each fed one cat.

If learners choose a picture that should not be possible in the target language, this may show non-targetlike interpretation.

---

### 7.4 Context–Sentence Matching Tasks

In this kind of task, learners read a short story and then decide whether a sentence or answer fits the story.

For example, in the French discontinuous interrogative study, learners may read about two people:

- one person was famous in the past
- another person is famous now

Then they decide which person is an appropriate answer to a French question.

This tests whether learners know the special meaning restriction of the French construction.

---

## 8. What Should You Listen for in the Presentations?

When listening to the presentations, focus on five questions.

### 8.1 What Is the Interface Problem?

Ask:

> What grammatical form is being studied?  
> What meaning is connected to that form?

| Study | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Slabakova (2003) | bare verb vs. progressive | completed vs. ongoing event |
| Marsden (2009) | Japanese word order | possible vs. impossible scope interpretation |
| Dekydtspotter & Sprouse (2001) | French continuous vs. discontinuous wh-phrases | different interpretations of the wh-question |

---

### 8.2 What Is the L1–L2 Difference?

Ask:

> Does the learner’s L1 express the same meaning differently?

Possible differences include:

- the L2 has a form that the L1 does not have
- the L1 and L2 use similar forms with different meanings
- one language allows a meaning that another language blocks
- the L2 construction is completely new for the learner

---

### 8.3 What Meaning Is Unavailable?

Ask:

> Is there an interpretation that learners must reject?

This is very important. If learners know that a sentence cannot have a certain meaning, this tells us something about their deep grammatical knowledge.

---

### 8.4 What Task Did the Researchers Use?

Ask:

> How did the researchers test learners’ interpretation?

Common tasks include:

- truth value judgment task
- acceptability judgment task
- picture selection task
- context–sentence matching task
- production task

---

### 8.5 What Do the Results Tell Us?

Ask:

> Did learners become targetlike?  
> Did proficiency matter?  
> Was there L1 transfer?  
> Did learners acquire meanings that were not directly taught?

These questions connect the studies to larger SLA issues.

---

## 9. Big Picture of Chapter 10

The main message of Chapter 10 is:

> L2 acquisition is not only about learning grammatical forms.  
> It is also about learning how grammatical forms create meanings.

Syntax–semantics interface research shows that L2 learners may acquire very subtle interpretive knowledge. Sometimes they learn meanings that are not explicitly taught.

At the same time, learning these mappings is not easy. Learners may transfer L1 patterns, accept interpretations that are not possible in the L2, or know a grammatical form before fully understanding its meaning.

The guiding question for this chapter is:

> How do L2 learners learn the hidden meaning consequences of grammatical form?

## 10.1 Types of Meaning  
Meaning in language has 5 interconnected layers—all work together to convey a complete message.  

### Core Types & Examples  
| Meaning Type | Definition | Key Example (from Chapter) | L2 Learning Tip |
|--------------|------------|-----------------------------|-----------------|
| **Lexical** | Literal, primary meaning of individual words (stored in mental lexicon). | English *cat* = Italian *gatto* (both = "small furry animal"); *tea* = drink/dried leaves/evening meal (UK). | Map L2 words to existing concepts (e.g., *gatto* → "cat") or learn new lexical meanings (e.g., *tea* = "evening meal"). |
| **Phrasal (Sentential) Semantic** | Compositional (calculated by combining words + rules) — can have multiple interpretations. | English: *Cats were exterminated* = "some cats" or "all cats"; Italian: *Gatti sono stati sterminati* = only "some cats." | Watch for cross-linguistic differences in how plural nouns/word order shape meaning. |
| **Grammatical** | Encoded in inflectional morphology (tense, agreement) — adds structural info. | *Jane eats* (present habitual) vs. *Jane ate* (past completed/habitual); -ed = past, -s = 3SG agreement. | Master grammatical morphemes to avoid meaning gaps (e.g., omitting -ed = unclear time). |
| **Discourse-Related** | Depends on prior context (links sentence to conversation). | Question: *Does Jane like sushi?* Answer: *Sushi, she eats; sashimi, she adores* (fronting = topic focus). | Pay attention to how context shapes word order (e.g., fronted objects = topic). |
| **Pragmatic** | Inferential (implied, not direct) — relies on context/Gricean maxims. | *Bonnie ate some strawberries* = "some but not all" (pragmatic) vs. "some possibly all" (logical); *Today is Tuesday* (denotation changes daily). | Learn to infer implied meaning (e.g., "some" ≠ "all" in English). |

### Integration Example: *Mona likes tea*  
- Lexical: *Mona* = name, *likes* = "feels positive toward," *tea* = "drink."  
- Phrasal Semantic: *Mona* (Experiencer) → *likes* → *tea* (Theme) = "Mona favors tea."  
- Grammatical: -s = 3SG agreement, present tense = habitual.  
- Discourse-Related: Neutral word order = new statement (not a response).  
- Pragmatic: Inferred = "Mona likes to drink tea" (not "watch tea").


## 10.2 Mismatches at the Syntax–Semantics Interface  
**Key Idea**: All languages convey the same universal meanings (e.g., "habitual past"), but use different forms to encode them — this "mismatch" causes L2 errors.  

### What Is a Mismatch?  
- No missing meanings — just different *form-meaning links* (e.g., English uses simple past for habitual past; Spanish uses Imperfect).  
- L2 Challenge: Unlearn L1 mappings and adopt L2-specific ones (not just memorize words).  

### Two Core Mismatches (with Examples)  
#### 1. Aspectual Tenses (English vs. Spanish)  
Universal semantic primitives: One-time completed event, habitual event, ongoing event.  

| Language | Form | Mapped Meanings | Example |
|----------|------|------------------|---------|
| English | Simple Past | One-time completed + habitual | *Felix robbed people* (habitual) / *Felix robbed a person* (one-time) |
| English | Past Progressive | Only ongoing | *Felix was robbing when police arrived* |
| Spanish | Preterit (-ó) | Only one-time completed | *Guillermo robó* = "Guillermo robbed someone once" |
| Spanish | Imperfect (-aba) | Habitual + ongoing | *Guillermo robaba* = "Guillermo robbed habitually" / "was robbing" |

- L2 Error: Spanish L1 learners say *\*Felix was robbing people* (to mean habitual) — transfers Spanish Imperfect’s "habitual" mapping to English past progressive (only ongoing).  

#### 2. Politeness Marking (English vs. German/Bulgarian/Russian)  
| Language | Polite Form (Singular Addressee) | Informal Form | English Equivalent |
|----------|----------------------------------|---------------|--------------------|
| German | *Sie* (2PL pronoun) | *du* (2SG) | "Sir/Madam" (lexical) |
| Bulgarian | *Vie* (2PL) | *ti* (2SG) | "Excuse me, Sir" |
| Russian | *vy* (2PL) | *ty* (2SG) | "Miss" |
| English | No morphological marker | *you* (all uses) | "Excuse me, do you know the time?" |

- L2 Error: German L1 learners say *\*Do Sie know the time?* — transfers German’s plural pronoun for politeness to English (uses lexical terms instead).


## 10.3 L2 Acquisition of Syntax–Semantics Mismatches  
**Key Idea**: Mismatches are acquirable! Learners transfer L1 mappings initially but adopt target L2 links with proficiency.  

### Study 1: Slabakova (2003) — Bulgarian L1 → English Aspect  
- Mismatch: English bare verbs (e.g., *I saw Mary cross*) entail completion ([+perfective] feature); Bulgarian equivalents do not.  
- Participants: 112 Bulgarian L2 English learners (low → advanced) + native controls.  
- Tasks: Production (check inflection knowledge) + Truth Value Judgment Task (TVJT) — judge if sentences match stories.  

| Story Context | English Test Sentence | Expected Judgment | Why? |
|---------------|-------------------------|--------------------|------|
| Matt ate half a cake (incomplete) | *I observed Matt eat a cake* | False | Bare verb = completion required. |
| Matt ate half a cake (incomplete) | *I observed Matt eating a cake* | True | -ing = no completion required. |
| Alicia ate a whole cake (complete) | *I watched Alicia eat a cake* | True | Bare verb = completion matches. |

- Results:  
  - Advanced learners: 95% accuracy on untaught [+perfective] feature (even more than natives).  
  - Intermediate learners: Improved with proficiency (68% → 83% → 95%).  
- Takeaway: Syntax comes before semantics — learners first master English’s "impoverished" verb morphology, then the linked [+perfective] meaning.  

### Study 2: Ionin et al. — Genericity (English vs. Spanish/Portuguese)  
- Mismatch: English uses bare plurals for generic meaning (*Tigers eat meat*) and definite plurals for specific (*The tigers eat meat*); Spanish uses definite plurals for both (*Los tigres comen carne* = generic + specific).  
- Results:  
  - Initial L1 transfer: Spanish L1 learners overuse *the* for generic (*\*The tigers eat meat*); English L1 learners use bare plurals in Spanish (*\*Tigres comen carne*).  
  - Advanced learners: Retreat to target — Spanish L1 uses English bare plurals; English L1 uses Spanish definite plurals.  
- Takeaway: Identifying ungrammatical forms (e.g., Spanish bare plurals) is easier than mastering all interpretations.


## 10.4 Poverty of the Stimulus (POS) in Semantics  
**Key Idea**: Learners acquire "unavailable meanings" (e.g., a sentence *can’t* mean X) without explicit teaching — Universal Grammar (UG) guides them.  

### What Is POS?  
- Two similar sentences: One has two meanings, the other has one (missing meaning = unavailable).  
- Input never shows "this sentence can’t mean X" — learners use UG to infer constraints.  

### Study 1: Spanish Impersonal *se*  
- POS Property: *se* + Imperfect = two meanings (generic: "people in general" + specific: "we"); *se* + Preterit = only specific ("we").  
- Example:  
  - *Se comía bien en casa de la abuela* = "People ate well" / "We ate well."  
  - *Se comió bien en casa de la abuela* = only "We ate well" (generic = unavailable).  
- Results: Advanced L2 Spanish learners (L1 English) = 85% accurate in rejecting generic for Preterit — UG guides interpretation.  

### Study 2: Japanese Distributive Quantifiers  
- POS Property: Canonical SOV order (*Dareka-ga dono hon-mo yonda*) = only S > O scope ("one person read all books"); scrambled OSV order = two scopes (S > O + O > S).  
- Results:  
  - Korean L1 learners (L1 = same as Japanese) = 95% accurate (transfer).  
  - English L1 learners (L1 = allows O > S in canonical order) = half mastered the POS constraint (rejected O > S for SOV) — UG overcomes transfer.  

- Takeaway: POS knowledge is acquirable via UG, even in adult L2 learning.


## 10.5 Meaning of Novel Constructions  
**Key Idea**: Novel constructions (no L1 equivalent) require learning syntax first — semantic constraints follow via universal mechanisms.  

### What Is a Novel Construction?  
- L2 structure with no direct L1 equivalent (e.g., French discontinuous interrogatives — English can’t split *wh*-phrases).  

### French Continuous vs. Discontinuous Interrogatives  
| Construction Type | Example | Allowable Interpretations of *de célèbre* ("famous") |
|--------------------|---------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Continuous | *Qui de célèbre fumait dans les années 70?* | Past (Al Green) + present (Oprah Winfrey) celebrities |
| Discontinuous | *Qui fumait de célèbre dans les années 70?* | Only past celebrities (Al Green) |

- Why? Syntax determines meaning:  
  - Continuous: *de célèbre* = above past tense → past/present fame.  
  - Discontinuous: *de célèbre* = below past tense → only past fame.  

### Study: Dekydtspotter & Sprouse (2001)  
- Participants: English L1 learners of French (intermediate → advanced).  
- Task: Judge if answers (Al Green/Oprah) fit questions (continuous/discontinuous).  
- Results:  
  - Advanced learners: 90% accuracy (rejected Oprah for discontinuous questions) — matched natives.  
- Takeaway: Once learners master the construction’s syntax, universal semantic rules (not input) guide meaning.


## 10.6 Conjuring Up Something from Nothing  
**Key Idea**: Learners acquire dedicated morphological markers (e.g., English articles *the/a*) for semantic features their L1 encodes via non-morphological means (context, word order).  

### Core Features: Definiteness vs. Specificity  
| Feature | Definition | Example |
|---------|------------|---------|
| Definiteness | Shared knowledge (speaker + hearer know the referent) | *The car was red* (car mentioned earlier). |
| Specificity | Speaker knows the referent (hearer doesn’t) | *Jill wants to marry a Canadian* (she has someone specific in mind). |

### Article Choice Parameter (Ionin et al., 2004)  
- Languages with two articles use one of two settings:  
  1. Definiteness (English): *the* = definite, *a* = indefinite.  
  2. Specificity (Samoan): *le* = specific, *se* = nonspecific.  
- L2 Prediction: Learners from article-less L1s (Russian/Korean) fluctuate between settings before settling on target.  

### Study Results: Russian/Korean L1 → English Articles  
- Fluctuation:  
  - Definite NPs: 79% *the* for specific definites vs. 57% for nonspecific (overused *a*).  
  - Indefinite NPs: 84% *a* for nonspecific vs. 54% for specific (overused *the*).  
- Proficiency Effect: Advanced learners showed less fluctuation.  

- L2 Challenges:  
  1. Feature overlap (definiteness ≈ specificity) → confusion.  
  2. No L1 morphology to transfer → test universal settings.  
  3. Context dependence → track speaker-hearer shared knowledge.


## Chapter 10 Key Takeaways for L2 Learners  
1. Meaning is multilayered — focus on form-meaning links, not just words.  
2. Mismatches and POS properties are acquirable via UG and proficiency.  
3. Initial L1 transfer is normal — target mappings emerge with exposure.  
4. Syntax often precedes semantics — master structure first, then meaning constraints.  
5. Pragmatic and discourse meaning require context practice (not just rules).  

Use these notes to connect theory to your own L2 learning — notice how you navigate form-meaning mappings in your target language!
