## CHAPTER 8: ACQUISITION OF SYNTAX  
**Core Goal**: Understand how L2 learners acquire syntactic structure (word order, movement) and whether UG still guides this process—especially for features not present in the L1.  


### 8.1 The Evolution of the Parameter Concept  
Parameters (language-specific options in UG) have shifted from "switches" to feature-based bundles—shaping how we study L2 syntax:  

#### 1. Early Parameter View (1980s–1990s)  
- **Idea**: Parameters are "on/off switches" that trigger clusters of unrelated constructions. For example, the **Null Subject Parameter (NSP)** was thought to link:  
  - Null subjects (*Mangia una mela* = "He/she eats an apple") .  
  - Null expletives (*Piove* = "It rains") .  
  - Postverbal subjects (*Ha telefonato uno studente* = "A student called") .  
- **Problem**: L2 studies (White 1985) found no cluster—Spanish learners of English fixed null subjects first, but postverbal subjects never followed.  

#### 2. Modern Parameter View (2000s–present)  
- **Shift to Features**: Parameters are now bundles of formal features (phonological, syntactic, semantic) on lexical items . For example:  
  - English past tense (-ed) = [+past] + [+perfective aspect] .  
  - Mandarin has no overt [past] feature—L2 learners must "reassemble" it onto a morpheme .  
- **Key Question**: Can L2 learners add/unlearn uninterpretable features (e.g., agreement, case) not present in the L1?  


### 8.2 Core Debate: Deficit vs. Full Representation  
The two dominant views explain L2 syntax challenges in fundamentally different ways:  

| **Representational Deficit Hypotheses** | **Full Functional Representation** |
|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Claim**: Uninterpretable features not in the L1 can’t be acquired (e.g., Hawkins & Chan 1997, Tsimpli & Dimitrakopoulou 2007) . | **Claim**: Nativelike syntax is possible—challenges come from mapping features to surface forms, not missing representations (e.g., Schwartz & Sprouse 1996) . |
| **Example**: Chinese learners of English can’t master wh-movement island constraints (no wh-movement in L1) . | **Example**: Chinese learners of English *can* reject wh-island violations (e.g., *Who did you wonder whether she met?*)—proves UG access . |
| **Explains**: Persistent errors (e.g., resumptive pronouns in English relative clauses) . | **Explains**: Variability (e.g., omitting V2 in German but mastering it with input) . |


### 8.3 Acquisition of Word Order  
Word order (SVO vs. SOV) is one of the first syntactic properties L2 learners adjust—and a test for transfer:  

#### 1. Canonical Word Order (SVO vs. SOV)  
- **Transfer Evidence**:  
  - Romance (SVO) learners of German (SOV) initially use SVO (Meisel et al. 1981) .  
  - Korean/Turkish (SOV) learners of German retain SVO later than Romance learners (Vainikka & Young-Scholten 1994) .  
- **Acquisition Path**: Learners first transfer L1 word order, then adjust to L2 order with input (e.g., Greek learners of Turkish master SOV by intermediate level—Papadopoulou et al. 2011) .  

#### 2. Non-Canonical Word Order (e.g., Turkish Object-Initial)  
- **Challenge**: Non-canonical order (e.g., Turkish *gazete-ler-i çocuk oku-du* = "The newspapers the child read") requires combining word order with case (Accusative -i) .  
- **Finding**: Intermediate learners struggle—only advanced learners master it (Papadopoulou et al. 2011) .  
- **Why**: Interaction of features (word order + case) increases complexity—supports the **Feature Reassembly Hypothesis** .  


### 8.4 Acquisition of Verb Movement  
Verb movement (e.g., V2 in German, verb raising over adverbs in French) depends on uninterpretable features—and is harder than word order:  

#### 1. Verb Raising (French vs. English)  
- **Linguistic Contrast**: French verbs move over adverbs (*Jean embrasse souvent Marie* = "John kisses often Mary"), while English verbs stay in VP (*John often kisses Mary*) .  
- **L2 Challenge**: Francophone learners of English initially transfer verb raising (e.g., *John kisses often Mary*)—even after instruction (White 1990) .  
- **Key Finding**: Instruction on one property (e.g., adverb placement) doesn’t generalize to others (e.g., question formation)—no parameter cluster .  

#### 2. V2 Parameter (German)  
- **Linguistic Rule**: The finite verb must be 2nd in German (*Im Park hat er sie gefunden* = "In the park had he her found") .  
- **Acquisition Timeline**:  
  - Beginners violate V2 in 49% of cases (Wahlstrom McKay 2001) .  
  - Advanced learners master it (Conradie 2006; Prévost 1999) .  
- **Why the Delay**: V2’s "signature" (non-subject initial + verb 2nd) is rare in input (1.2% of child-directed speech—Yang 2004) .  


### 8.5 Acquisition of Wh-Movement  
Wh-movement (e.g., *What did John buy?*) and its constraints (islands) are critical for testing UG access—thanks to **Poverty of the Stimulus**:  

#### 1. Wh-Movement Parameter (In-Situ vs. Movement)  
- **L1 Transfer**:  
  - Chinese (wh-in-situ: *Hufei mai-le shenme?* = "Hufei bought what?") learners of English initially omit wh-movement (*John bought what?*) but fix it with input (Li 1998) .  
  - English learners of Chinese adopt wh-in-situ by advanced levels (White & Juffs 1998) .  

#### 2. Island Constraints (Ungrammatical Extractions)  
- **Islands**: Structures from which wh-movement is forbidden (e.g., wh-islands: *Who did you wonder whether she met?* = ungrammatical) .  
- **Poverty of the Stimulus**: Learners never hear ungrammatical islands—rejecting them proves UG access .  
- **Key Evidence**:  
  - Chinese/Indonesian learners of English reject wh-island violations at rates above chance (Martohardjono 1993) .  
  - Advanced learners distinguish "weak" (??*Which car did you spread the rumor that the neighbor stole?*) and "strong" (*Which neighbor did you spread the rumor that stole a car?*) violations—matching natives (Table 8.1) .  

#### 3. Resumptive Pronouns (Test for Deficits)  
- **Linguistic Contrast**: English forbids resumptives (*This is the girl I gave a present to her*), but Chinese/Greek allow them .  
- **Debate**:  
  - **Deficit View**: Chinese learners never fully reject resumptives (Hawkins & Chan 1997) .  
  - **Full Representation View**: Spanish learners who dislike resumptives in L1 reject them in L2 English (Leal Méndez & Slabakova 2014)—individual differences, not deficits .  


### 8.6 Conclusions  
1. **Parameters = Feature Bundles**: The "switch" view is outdated—syntax acquisition now focuses on reassembling features on lexical items .  
2. **Full Representation Is Possible**: Advanced learners master complex syntax (e.g., German V2, wh-island constraints) via UG + input .  
3. **Poverty of the Stimulus Proves UG**: Rejecting ungrammatical structures (e.g., wh-islands) can’t come from input—must rely on innate principles .

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Movement & placement**: Many word-order differences (adverb/negation position, subject–aux inversion, clause-final verbs) are **movement-driven**; learners’ L1 settings predict early **placement errors**.
- **Dependencies & constraints**: **Wh-movement** and **relative clauses** create **filler–gap** dependencies; **islands** constrain movement—use these to test whether L2 grammars encode **abstract constraints**.
- **Competing accounts**:
  - **Representational deficit**: Certain **functional features** (e.g., strong movement triggers) are **absent or unrecoverable** → persistent optionality even with time/practice.  
  - **Full representation + processing** (e.g., SSH): Target-like features **are present**, but **time pressure** or **memory load** yields **shallower** online structure-building, leading to slower RTs or more errors under speed.

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1, 45 min)

#### 1) Word order & movement diagnostics (V-to-T, V2, head direction)
- **Key notes**:  
  - **V-to-T**: French allows finite verbs to appear before adverbs/negation (V raises), English typically doesn’t (finite main verbs stay below Adv/Neg; auxiliaries move).  
  - **V2**: Germanic V2 puts the finite verb in second position in **main** clauses (not the same in embedded clauses).  
  - **Head direction**: OV vs. VO / head-final vs. head-initial affects verb–object order and placement of particles/clauses.  
- **Class activity**: Label the **diagnostic region** in minimal pairs (e.g., *Jean (souvent) mange des pommes* vs. *John often eats apples* / *John eats often apples*).

#### 2) Long-distance dependencies (wh, relatives) & island constraints
- **Key notes**:  
  - **A′-movement** (wh/fronting) vs. **A-movement** (passive/raising).  
  - **Islands**: wh-island (embedded Q), complex NP (relative clauses / complements of nouns), subject islands.  
  - Use island violations to ask if L2 grammars respect **abstract constraints** (competence) even if online **reanalysis** is costly.  
- **Example**: Compare acceptability for wh-extraction from complements vs. from **island** positions.

#### 3) Competing accounts & predictions (deficit vs. processing)
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Deficit/failed features** → **persistent** misplacement or acceptance of ungrammatical extraction **even in untimed, highly explicit tasks**.
