- How **age of acquisition (AoA)** interacts with **input amount/quality**, **use**, and **education**.

## Key Chapter Goal  
Answer: *Does biology limit when we can acquire a first (L1) or second (L2) language natively?*  


## 4.1 The View from Biology  
### Core Idea  
Critical periods (biological "windows" for learning) are not unique to humans—they support the CPH for language.  

### Key Examples  
1. **Cross-Species Precedents**  
   - **Songbirds**: Zebra finches learn normal songs only if exposed to adult songs by 50 days old; canaries relearn annually (Nottebohm 1984).  
   - **Cats**: Kittens deprived of visual input before 8 weeks have permanent blindness (Hubel & Wiesel 1963)—parallels human brain plasticity decline.  

2. **Human Neural Development**  
   - **Plasticity**: Children recover language after left hemisphere damage; adults do not (Lenneberg 1967).  
   - **Lateralization**: Language localizes to left hemisphere (Broca’s/Wernicke’s areas) by puberty—CPH ends when lateralization completes.  

3. **Lenneberg’s (1967) CPH Evidence**  
   - Feral child *Genie* (discovered at 13, no language input): Never mastered complex syntax.  


## 4.2 The Two Positions in L2 Acquisition  
### Debate: Does CPH Apply to L2?  
| Position 1: CPH Applies to L2 | Position 2: CPH Does Not Apply to L2 |  
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------|  
| **Claim**: L2 nativelikeness declines sharply after puberty. | **Claim**: Age effects are gradual (no puberty cutoff) and input-driven. |  
| **Example**: Johnson & Newport (1989): 46 Chinese/Korean L2 English learners—AoA <15 = linear score decline; AoA >15 = flat scores. | **Example**: Bialystok & Hakuta (1994): 2,000+ immigrants—late learners (AoA >20) with high education/residence scored near-native. |  


## 4.3 Modular Sensitive Periods (Not Global Nativelikeness)  
### Core Idea  
No single "CPH for L2"—different grammar components have distinct biological windows.  

| Grammar Component | Sensitive Period End | Key Example |  
|--------------------|-----------------------|-------------|  
| Phonology (accent) | ~6–8 years | Birdsong 2005: L2 Spanish learners (AoA >12) = non-native vowels. |  
| Morphology (tense/agreement) | ~10–12 years | DeKeyser 2000: L2 French learners (AoA >10) struggled with past tense *-é*. |  
| Syntax (phrase structure) | ~15–18 years | White 2003: L2 English learners (AoA >20) mastered wh-movement. |  
| Semantics (meaning) | Nearly open | Kroll & Stewart 1994: No age effect on L2 semantic priming. |  

### Example: Hyltenstam & Abrahamsson (2003)  
L2 Swedish learners (AoA 11–22): Near-native syntax but non-native accent—proves modularity.  


## 4.4 L1-L2 as "Communicating Vessels"  
### Core Idea  
L1 and L2 share cognitive resources—knowledge transfers between them (L1→L2 or L2→L1).  

### Key Examples  
1. **L1→L2 Transfer**: Flege (1995): L1 English/L2 Spanish learners (AoA >12) transferred English /r/ (retroflex) to Spanish /r/ (trill).  
2. **L2→L1 Attrition**: Kupisch & Rothman (2011): L1 German child bilinguals (L2 English-dominant) made L1 gender errors.  


## 4.5 Bilinguals: Not "Two Monolinguals in One Mind"  
### Core Idea  
Bilinguals have **integrated grammars** (shared representations for overlapping features).  

### Evidence Against "Two Monolinguals"  
1. **Code-Switching**: Poplack (1980): Spanish-English bilinguals only switch at shared syntactic boundaries (e.g., *Yo saw the movie* = okay; *\*I vi la película* = not okay).  
2. **Cross-Linguistic Priming**: Kroll & Stewart (1994): L1 *casa* (Spanish) speeded L2 *house* (English) recognition.  


## 4.6 & 4.7 The Importance of Input  
### Core Idea  
Sensitive periods = "opportunity windows"; input = "fuel"—no input = no acquisition.  

### Key Examples  
1. **Input Necessity**: Victor of Aveyron (feral child, no input) = no complex language.  
2. **Input Quality**: Long (1996): L2 learners with interactive input (feedback) mastered past tense *-ed* 2x faster than passive input.  
3. **Indirect Evidence**:  
   - **Fossilization**: Adult L2 English learners (no feedback) = persistent 3SG *-s* errors (*\*He walk*).  
   - **Creoles**: Hawaiian Creole English (HCE): Children turned simplified pidgin input into a full grammar (e.g., tense marking: *He bin work* = past).  


## Chapter 4 Takeaways  
1. Biology (sensitive periods) sets limits, but input determines if those limits matter.  
2. L2 acquisition: Phonology/morphology are most age-vulnerable; syntax/semantics are more accessible late.  
3. Bilinguals have integrated grammars—L1 and L2 interact dynamically.

## CHAPTER 4: THE CRITICAL PERIOD HYPOTHESIS (CPH)  
**Core Goal**: Understand if there’s a "critical age" for second language (L2) acquisition—and why some learners achieve nativelike skills while others don’t.  


### 4.1 The Biological Roots of CPH  
CPH originates from biology: many skills (e.g., vision) have a "critical period"—early life window when development is optimal, after which it’s irreversible.  

- **Key Example**: Hubel & Wiesel (1970) deprived kittens of one eye’s use early in life—they never developed binocular vision.  
- **Language Link**: Lenneberg (1967) argued language has a critical period (ages 2–puberty) tied to brain lateralization (language functions localizing to one brain hemisphere). After puberty:  
  - Lexicon (vocabulary) can still be learned.  
  - Nativelike grammar mastery is impossible.  
- **Evidence**: Cases like Genie (isolated until age 13) – she never fully acquired English grammar, even with later input.  

- **Update**: Modern neuroscience shows **brain plasticity** (ability to change) persists into adulthood—so researchers now talk of "sensitive periods" (optimal, not irreversible) instead of strict critical periods.  


### 4.2 Two Sides of the L2 CPH Debate  
Lenneberg’s CPH was for first language (L1) acquisition. For L2, researchers disagree:  

| "Critical/Sensitive Period" Proponents (Long, DeKeyser, Hyltenstam) | "Nuanced Attainment" Proponents (Birdsong, Montrul, Slabakova) |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Adults/teens can’t reach full nativelike L2 grammar (phonology, syntax, processing). | Nativelike skills are possible for some learners—depends on input quality/quantity, proficiency, and which grammar module (e.g., morphosyntax vs. phonology) is tested. |
| Early L2 learners (before 12) rarely reach nativelike levels either (contrary to old assumptions). | "Sensitive periods" exist for *some* grammar parts (e.g., pronunciation) but not all (e.g., semantics). |

- **Common Ground**: Both now use "sensitive period" (flexible) instead of "critical period" (strict).  


### 4.3 Global Nativelikeness vs. Module-Specific Sensitive Periods  
Is full nativelike L2 mastery impossible after puberty? Studies show a mixed picture:  

#### 1. Evidence for Sensitive Periods (Global Nativelikeness)  
- **Johnson & Newport (1989)**: Tested 46 Korean/Chinese L2 English learners.  
  - Strong negative correlation: Younger arrival age (AoA 3–15) = better grammar scores (r=-.87).  
  - After AoA 15: No correlation (scores depended on individual traits, not age).  
- **Abrahamsson & Hyltenstam (2009)**: 195 Spanish L2 Swedish learners.  
  - Only 5% of learners who started after 12 were perceived as native.  
  - None of the teen/adult learners scored nativelike on *all* tests (phonetics, grammar, semantics).  
- **Abrahamsson (2012)**: Similar to above—younger AoA (before 15) = better grammar/VOT (sound distinction) scores; adults had big variability.  

#### 2. Evidence for Module-Specific Success (Against Strict CPH)  
Some learners reach nativelike skills in *specific grammar areas*:  
- **Donaldson (2011)**: Near-native French learners used "Left Dislocation" (e.g., *Marie, elle vient* = "Mary, she is coming") nativelike—coordinating syntax and discourse (rarely taught explicitly).  
- **Montrul & Slabakova (2003)**: Advanced English L2 Spanish learners mastered subtle tense-aspect meanings (e.g., *se comía-IMPF* = "one ate" vs. *se comió-PRET* = "we ate")—a "Poverty of the Stimulus" case (not teachable via input alone).  

- **Takeaway**: Full global nativelikeness is rare after puberty—but nativelike skills in morphosyntax/semantics are possible.  


### 4.4 L1-L2 Interaction: Communicating Vessels  
L1 and L2 influence each other—like liquid in "communicating vessels" (more L1 = less L2, or vice versa):  

- **Flege’s Speech Learning Model**: L1 and L2 sounds share a "phonological space"—they mutually interfere.  
  - Example: Yeni-Komshian et al. (2000): Korean L2 English learners who arrived before 12 had a negative correlation (better L2 pronunciation = worse L1 pronunciation).  
- **Counterevidence**: Bylund et al. (2012): Spanish L2 Swedish learners who scored well in L1 also scored well in L2 (no interference).  

- **Why the Difference?** Interference is stronger for *pronunciation* (phonology) than for grammar/semantics.  


### 4.5 Bilingualism: Not "Two Monolinguals in One Mind"  
Bilinguals’ brains process L1 and L2 differently than monolinguals:  
- **Key Trait**: L1 and L2 are *constantly activated* (even when using one language)—bilinguals need to "select" the right language.  
  - Example: Spanish-English bilinguals use more English passives after hearing Spanish passives (Hartsuiker et al. 2004).  
- **Brain Overlap**: Bilinguals use mostly the same brain areas for L1 and L2—L2 can even change L1 (e.g., Welsh-English bilinguals unconsciously access Welsh word order when reading English).  

- **Implications**: Comparing bilinguals to monolinguals is unfair—bilinguals’ competence is "different, not deficient."  


### 4.6 Input Quality/Quantity Trumps Age  
Age of acquisition (AoA) isn’t the only factor—input (exposure to L2) is equally critical:  

- **Quantity Matters**:  
  - Simultaneous bilinguals (L1+L2 from birth) with unequal input have stronger skills in their "dominant" language (Thordardottir 2015).  
  - Heritage speakers (L1 = home language, L2 = majority language) lose L1 proficiency if input decreases (e.g., less home L1 use after starting school)—their L1 skills can match adult L2 learners (Montrul 2009).  

- **Quality Matters**:  
  - High-quality input = diverse (home/school/professional registers), communicatively relevant, and comprehensible.  
  - Example: Jia & Aronson (2003): Young Mandarin-English learners (5–9) had richer peer interaction = faster L2 dominance vs. teens (12–16).  

- **Muñoz (2014)**: For L2 English learners in Spain, input (hours of lessons, time abroad) predicted oral performance better than AoA.  


### 4.7 Input’s Importance: Even for Monolinguals  
Input affects L1 acquisition too—showing input, not just age, drives mastery:  
- **Miller & Schmitt (2010)**: Mexican Spanish (clear plural markers) vs. Chilean Spanish (unreliable plural markers). Mexican kids learned plurals faster.  
- **Meisel et al. (2011)**: French adults struggle with formal interrogatives (e.g., *Quand arrive le train?*)—they rarely heard them as kids (only learned at school, like an L2).  


### 4.8 Conclusions  
1. **Sensitive, Not Critical**: Language has "sensitive periods"—optimal for phonology (pronunciation) early, but morphosyntax/semantics can be mastered later.  
2. **Input Is Key**: Quantity/quality of L2 input can override age effects (e.g., heritage speakers with strong input maintain L1 skills).  
3. **Bilinguals Are Different**: Their L1/L2 interaction means nativelike = "matching native bilinguals," not monolinguals.  
4. **Teaching Takeaway**: Adults can achieve nativelike skills in grammar/semantics—focus on high-quality input, not just "early start."  


### Key Terms  
- Critical Period: Early irreversible window for development (old term).  
- Sensitive Period: Flexible, optimal window (modern term).  
- Brain Plasticity: Brain’s ability to change with experience.  
- Heritage Speaker: Learner with L1 = home language, L2 = majority language.  
- Poverty of the Stimulus: Learning a grammar feature that can’t be deduced from input alone.

### 🧩 Core content (gentle, big-picture)

#### 1) What the CPH says (and softer variants)
- **Strong (hard) CPH**: after a specific age window, **native-like** attainment is no longer possible.  
- **Sensitive-period view**: age effects are **gradual**; later starters can improve a lot, but **probability** of fully native-like outcomes declines with AoA.  
- **Friendly takeaway**: Age matters, but **how** it matters depends on *what you measure* and *how carefully you measure it*.

#### 2) What outcomes are we talking about?
- **Phonology (accent)**: shows **robust age effects**—late starters often retain a detectable accent even with long residence.  
- **Morphosyntax/semantics**: more **mixed**; late near-natives exist, especially with high-quality input, education, and deliberate practice.  
- **Pragmatics/discourse**: can be challenging well past childhood due to **context-dependent** mappings and lower salience.

#### 3) Measuring “ultimate attainment” (why methods matter)
- **Tasks**: global ratings (accent/intelligibility), **grammaticality judgments**, sentence interpretation, timed processing measures.  
- **Baselines**: who counts as a “native” control? Are items **balanced** and **independent** of vocabulary/background knowledge?  
- **Confounds**: **education**, **socioeconomic factors**, **exposure**, **use**, **motivation**, **aptitude**—all can inflate or mask age effects.  
- **Friendly rule**: the stronger the design (clean baselines, good controls, reliable measures), the **clearer** the age pattern you can interpret.

#### 4) AoA versus input/use (don’t mix them up)
- **AoA** is not the same as **time in the L2**. Two late starters with the same AoA can diverge if one gets **richer input**, **more use**, or **formal schooling**.  
- **Prediction**: Under matched input/education, age effects will still appear—especially in **phonology**—but may be **smaller** in some **morphosyntactic** domains.

#### 5) Shapes of the function (what curves look like)
- **Step-like** (hard CPH): sharp drop after a boundary (e.g., puberty).  
- **Continuous** (sensitive period): gradual decline with **no sharp cliff**.  
- **Hybrid**: steep early decline that **flattens** later—common in real datasets.

#### 6) Why subsystems differ
- **Phonology** depends on early-tuned **perceptual categories** and fine **motor patterns** → strong age effects.  
- **Morphosyntax/semantics** can be **explicitly taught**, are **rehearsable**, and benefit from **literacy** and **education** → more late success cases.  
- **Interfaces** (structure + context) add **processing load**, which can exacerbate age effects when **time pressure** is high.

#### 7) What would count as evidence—for or against?
- **Supports CPH**: a **monotonic AoA effect** remains after controlling input/use/education; late starters cluster **below** native range on demanding measures.  
- **Challenges CPH**: **documented late near-natives** meeting **strict performance criteria**; AoA slope weakens or disappears once **confounds** are rigorously controlled.  
- **Falsifier (hard CPH)**: many late starters consistently within **native distribution** on multiple **independent** measures.

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Not one outcome, many**: Age relates differently to **phonology** (large age effects), **morphosyntax** (mixed), and **semantics/pragmatics** (often learnable with input).
- **Input matters**: The **amount** (quantity) and **informativeness** (quality/diagnosticity) of input can **attenuate** age effects; poor or ambiguous input slows attainment.
- **Operationalization is destiny**: “Nativelikeness” depends on **what you measure** (accent ≠ grammar ≠ processing). Global ratings and local diagnostics can diverge.
- **Design before verdict**: Strong claims (e.g., maturational closing) require **controls** for use, education, SES, literacy, motivation, and **task demands**.

#### 1) What do Critical/Sensitive Period claims predict?
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Strong CPH**: maturational window closes → **discontinuity** in outcomes by AoA.  
  - **Sensitive period**: **continuous** decline with age, but learning remains possible.  
  - **Predictions**: later AoA → lower ultimate attainment **holding input constant**; the shape (break vs. slope) is empirical.

- **Key notes**:

  - **Task effects**: time pressure (online) reveals processing costs despite near-native **offline** accuracy.

- **Key notes**:
