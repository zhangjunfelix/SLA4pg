---

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Architecture**: Language knowledge is organized into modules (lexicon, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics) that interface with sound (PF) and meaning (LF).  
- **Scientific method**: Observe patterns → form hypotheses → design tests → revise models. SLA aims for explanations, not just description.  
- **Predictions**: Theories must generate concrete predictions (e.g., languages with rich agreement may allow null subjects).  
- **Theory-driven SLA**: Research questions stem from prior theory; outcomes support or challenge proposals.  
- **Teaching relevance**: Some modules (e.g., phonology) are harder to acquire post-childhood; others (syntax, semantics) remain learnable. This shapes pedagogy.  

---

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1)

#### 1. What is language? What is “knowing” a language? (Ch. 1.1)
- **Key notes**: Knowing a language involves phonological, morphological, syntactic, and semantic competence. Competence ≠ performance. SLA requires building an interlanguage system that maps onto these modules.  
- **Class activity**: Quick discussion: which part of your L2 (sounds, word order, vocabulary) do you find most challenging?

#### 2. The architecture of language (Ch. 1.2)
- **Key notes**: Language is modular—phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, with interfaces. Example: adjective–noun order differs between English (“red car”) and French (“voiture rouge”). Such differences predict challenges for learners.  
- **Diagram**: Instructor draws the architecture (PF ↔ Modules ↔ LF).  

#### 3. What has to be acquired in L2? (Ch. 1.3)
- **Key notes**: Learners must re-set features not present in their L1. Example: if L1 lacks articles, learners must acquire [+definiteness] features in L2.  
- **Discussion**: Is transfer always beneficial? Compare positive vs. negative transfer.  

#### 4. The scientific method in SLA (Ch. 1.4)
- **Key notes**: SLA research is theory-driven: hypotheses must be testable and falsifiable. Example: Hypothesis—learners of Italian (rich agreement, null subject) learning English will initially drop subjects. Prediction—subject omission in early English learner data.  
- **Activity**: Work in groups to formulate a simple SLA hypothesis, derive one prediction, and propose a test (e.g., grammaticality judgment).

### 🧪 Methods micro-lab (Session 2)
**Goal**: Practice turning a theoretical claim into a testable design.  
**Prompt**: “If L1 allows null subjects but L2 does not, beginners may omit subjects in L2 production.”  
- Prediction: beginners will omit more subjects.  
- Test: collect early learner writing/speech and compare subject use.
