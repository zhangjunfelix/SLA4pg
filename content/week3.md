## Week 3 — Processing & the Parser: How L2 is Used in Real Time


> **Anchor text**: Slabakova, R. (2016). *Second Language Acquisition*. **Chapter 3: The psychological reality of language in use**.

### ⭐ Overview
- What “processing” means: how the mind **builds meaning in real time** during listening/reading and plans **forms** during speaking.  
- Big ideas: **the parser**, timing, limited resources, and why learners sometimes rely on **easier cues** (semantics/discourse) over **costly structure** when time is tight.  
- What common **methods** actually measure (self-paced reading, eye-tracking, ERP) and how to read their basic signals.  
- Why this matters: classroom input and tasks can **shape processing** (e.g., highlighting small forms, pacing, repeated exposure).

---

1. Explain, in plain terms, what the **parser** does and why time/attention matter.

3. State one simple prediction about when L2 learners might rely more on **meaning cues** than **syntax** (and why).  
4. Suggest one **teaching move** that eases processing demands for a difficult form.

### 🧩 Core content (gentle, big-picture)

#### 1) What “processing” is (and isn’t)
- **Competence** is stored knowledge; **processing** is the on-the-fly construction of structure/meaning with limited time and attention.  
- The **parser** builds interpretations **incrementally**—word by word—sometimes making **early guesses** that must be revised.

**Try it:** Read quickly: *While the man hunted the deer ran into the woods.*  
Did you initially attach *the deer* as the object of *hunted*? That’s a **garden-path**—your parser made a fast guess, then had to **rebuild**.


#### 2) Common processing architectures (friendly snapshot)
- **Serial/modular** flavor: syntax builds first, semantics/pragmatics integrate a beat later.  
- **Interactive/constraint-based** flavor: multiple cues (lexical, frequency, discourse) compete **at once**.  
- **Good-Enough** idea: under load, the system may settle for a **plausible** parse, not a perfect one.

**Why this matters for L2:** If resources are tight, learners may lean more on **meaning/plausibility** and less on **fine-grained structure**—especially at lower proficiency or in noisy input.


#### 3) Methods you will see in papers (what the signals mean)
- **Self-Paced Reading (SPR):** word-by-word times. **Longer** times = more processing cost (e.g., at a disambiguating region).  
- **Eye-tracking:** where eyes land, **first-pass** times, and **regressions** (looking back) show difficulty and reanalysis.  
- **ERP (EEG):**  
  - **N400** (≈ semantic fit, lexical access)—larger when a word is less expected/meaning-incongruent.  
  - **P600** (≈ structural reanalysis/violation)—larger when structure clashes or must be repaired.

You don’t need formulas—just the **intuition**: bigger signal or slower time = **harder** at that moment.


#### 4) What tends to happen in L2 (and why)
- **Timing**: lexical access and integration can be **slower**, leaving **less time** for complex structure before the next word arrives.  
- **Cue weighting**: learners may **over-rely** on semantics/discourse (highly helpful) and **under-use** subtle morphosyntax (costly, low-salience).  
- **Proficiency & exposure**: with practice and input, structural cues become **faster**, and reliance re-balances.  
- **Task demands**: time pressure pushes **Good-Enough** outcomes; relaxed tasks allow **full structure** use.

**Friendly prediction:** If a structure is late-learned and **low-salience** (e.g., agreement endings), learners will rely more on **word order** or **plausibility** in fast reading.


#### 5) Small forms, big load
- **Functional morphology** (agreement, tense/aspect marking) can carry a lot of **grammatical work** in tiny pieces; it is perceptually **low-salience** and easy to miss online.  
- **Interfaces** (syntax↔semantics, syntax↔pragmatics) require coordinating **multiple systems** under time pressure → higher risk of **slowdowns** or **Good-Enough** interpretations.


#### 6) Classroom implications (processing-aware teaching)
- **Enhance salience** of small forms (bold/underline in reading cycles; brief auditory emphasis).  
- **Control pace** (shorter sentences first; chunking; guided pauses) to allow **structure** to “catch up.”  
- **Repeat exposure** to the **same form–meaning** mapping across varied but simple contexts.  
- **Minimal pair noticing** (e.g., *He run* vs. *He runs*) with quick meaning checks to tie form → interpretation.

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Incrementality**: The parser commits **early** to a structure based on probabilistic cues; strong priors can mislead (garden-paths) and require reanalysis.
- **Competence vs. performance**: A learner may **know** a structure (competence) yet still read it more slowly or err under time pressure (performance).
- **Cue-based retrieval**: Integration depends on retrieving the correct **dependency** from memory; **interference** occurs when distractors share features (e.g., number).
- **Good-enough processing**: Under load, the system may settle for **plausible** representations without full detail, increasing shallow-structure outcomes.
- **Measurement matters**: **Online** methods (SPR, eye-tracking, ERP) detect **time-course** effects that offline accuracy may miss.

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1)

#### 1) What does the parser do, and when?
- **Key notes**: Parsing is **incremental**—word-by-word commitments; ambiguity is resolved using lexical, syntactic, and discourse cues. Early commitments can create **garden-paths** (e.g., “While Anna dressed the baby…”) requiring reanalysis.
- **Class activity idea**: Read classic garden-path sentences aloud; predict where reanalysis will occur and why.

#### 2) Sources of difficulty: ambiguity & memory
- **Key notes**: Ambiguities (attachment, filler–gap, relative clauses) and **memory constraints** drive slowdowns. Long **dependencies** and **structural complexity** increase retrieval demands.
- **Example**: Subject vs. object relative clauses; the object relatives often incur larger integration costs, especially for L2 readers.

#### 3) Cue-based retrieval & interference
- **Key notes**: The system retrieves earlier material by matching **features** (e.g., number, gender). **Similarity-based interference** (distractors share features) produces **slowdowns** or **attraction errors** (e.g., “The key to the cabinets *are*…”).
- **Discussion**: Predict which L1→L2 pairings heighten interference for **agreement** dependencies.

#### 4) Competence vs. performance: L2 implications
- **Key notes**: Slower or less accurate **online** performance does not imply lack of competence. **Processing load**, not representational deficit, can explain delayed or shallow effects—especially under time pressure.
- **Activity**: For a phenomenon you know (e.g., verb movement or agreement), sketch both a **competence** account and a **processing** account. What data would differentiate them?

### 🧪 Methods micro-lab (Session 2)
**Goal**: Link a processing claim to an empirical signature.

Choose **one** design and complete the template.

- **Self-Paced Reading (SPR)**  
  - *Claim*: L2 readers experience **interference** in long-distance agreement.  
  - *Prediction*: Longer reading times at the **agreement region** when a distractor noun mismatches the subject’s number.  
  - *Measure*: Region-by-region RTs; compare **match vs. mismatch** conditions.

- **Eye-tracking during Reading**  
  - *Claim*: L2 readers show extended **regressions** in garden-paths.  
  - *Prediction*: Increased **first-pass** and **regression-path** times at the disambiguating region.  
  - *Measure*: First fixation, gaze duration, regression path, total time.

  - *Claim*: L2 detects **morpho-syntactic violations** but with delayed/attenuated responses.
