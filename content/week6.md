## CHAPTER 7: ACQUISITION OF (FUNCTIONAL) MORPHOLOGY  
**Core Goal**: Understand how L2 learners acquire functional morphology (the "heart of language variation")—and why production variability does not always signal incomplete grammatical knowledge {insert\_element\_9\_}.  


### 7.1 What Is Functional Morphology?  
Functional morphology is the core of cross-linguistic differences, with 3 non-negotiable aspects:  
1. **Morphophonological form**: Pronounceable (often a prefix/suffix/infix, e.g., English -s, -ed) {insert\_element\_10\_}.  
2. **Grammatical meaning**: Carries abstract features like tense (past/present), number (singular/plural), or gender (masculine/feminine) {insert\_element\_11\_}.  
3. **Syntactic consequences**: Regulates phrase movement (e.g., English -s signals verb stays in VP, while French verbs move past adverbs) {insert\_element\_12\_}.  

- **Critical Role**: Without functional morphology (e.g., omitting -s in *He walk*), sentences are ungrammatical—even if the message is understandable {insert\_element\_13\_}.  


### 7.2 Measuring Functional Morphology Knowledge  
Researchers rely on production and interpretation tasks to assess mastery—with key methodological considerations:  

#### 1. Obligatory Contexts (Production)  
- **Definition**: Environments where native speakers would use a specific morpheme (e.g., *two book___* requires plural -s; *I am go___* requires progressive -ing) {insert\_element\_14\_}.  
- **Calculation**:  
  - Raw count: Number of correct morphemes supplied.  
  - Percentage: (Correct supplies / Total obligatory contexts) × 100.  
  - **Why Both Matter**: Raw counts show total use; percentages let comparisons across learners/tasks {insert\_element\_15\_}.  

#### 2. Limitation of Production Alone  
- Learners may produce a morpheme correctly but misinterpret its meaning. For example, English-speaking children say *Mama Bear is touching her* but 50% interpret it as "Mama Bear is touching herself" {insert\_element\_16\_}.  
- **Fix**: Combine production with interpretation tasks (e.g., asking learners to choose which scenario matches a sentence with past tense -ed) {insert\_element\_17\_}.  

#### 3. Adjusting for Overuse: Stauble’s Method  
- Traditional accuracy (e.g., 5/10 = 50% for -ing) ignores overuse (e.g., 3 extra -ing in imperatives like *Sitting down!*).  
- **Adjustment**: Add overuse cases to the denominator (5/(10+3) = 38%) for a more realistic score {insert\_element\_18\_}.  


### 7.3 Early Morpheme Order Studies  
The first wave of L2 morphology research focused on whether L2 learners follow the same acquisition order as L1 children:  

#### 1. Foundational L1 Work: Brown (1973)  
- Tracked 3 English L1 children (Adam, Eve, Sarah) and found a fixed order of functor morpheme acquisition (e.g., progressive -ing before 3rd person -s) {insert\_element\_19\_}.  

#### 2. L2 Extensions: Dulay & Burt (1973, 1974)  
- **Creative Construction Hypothesis**: L2 children use innate grammar principles to build L2 knowledge—not just L1 transfer {insert\_element\_20\_}.  
- **Key Finding**: Spanish and Chinese L2 learners of English showed nearly identical morpheme accuracy patterns (e.g., low accuracy on possessive ’s, high on plural -s) {insert\_element\_21\_}.  

#### 3. Adult L2: Bailey et al. (1974)  
- Confirmed similar order: Progressive -ing, contractible copula (I’m) = early; possessive ’s, 3rd person -s = late {insert\_element\_22\_}.  

#### 4. Criticisms  
- Underestimated L1 influence (e.g., Japanese learners of English acquire plural -s faster than Spanish learners) {insert\_element\_23\_}.  
- Ignored meaning: Learners like Homer (Iranian L2 English) overused -ing for all tenses (e.g., *Sitting down!* for imperatives) — production accuracy masked interpretation gaps {insert\_element\_24\_}.  


### 7.4 Theoretical Hypotheses: Why L2 Morphology Is Hard  
Four major hypotheses explain L2 functional morphology challenges—split into "representational deficit" and "full functional representation" views:  

#### 1. Syntax-before-Morphology (White 2003)  
- **Claim**: Learners acquire syntactic properties of a functional category first (e.g., obligatory overt subjects) before mastering its morphological form (e.g., 3rd person -s) {insert\_element\_25\_}.  
- **Evidence**: Lardiere’s (1998a,b) study of Patty (Mandarin/Hokkien L1 → English L2):  
  - Patty omitted 3rd person -s (4.5% accuracy) and past tense -ed (34.5% accuracy) but used overt subjects (98% accuracy) and nominative case (100% accuracy) {insert\_element\_26\_}.  
  - Conclusion: Patty knew TP (tense phrase) syntax but struggled with morphological mapping {insert\_element\_27\_}.  

#### 2. Representational Deficit Hypotheses  
- **Claim**: Poor morphology production = impaired underlying syntactic features (only accessible if present in L1) {insert\_element\_28\_}.  
- **Example**: Hawkins & Liszka (2003) found Chinese L2 learners of English produced past tense -ed only 63% of the time (vs. German/Japanese learners’ 92%)—attributed to Chinese lacking TP (tense phrase) {insert\_element\_29\_}.  
- **Criticism**: Cannot explain why learners like Patty produce morphemes correctly some of the time (e.g., 34.5% past tense -ed) {insert\_element\_30\_}.  

#### 3. Missing Surface Inflection Hypothesis (MSIH)  
- **Claim**: No representational deficit—variability comes from mapping abstract syntactic features to surface morphology (e.g., accessing -s/-ed in production) {insert\_element\_31\_}.  
- **Evidence**:  
  - Erdem (Turkish L1 → English L2): Used overt subjects (sign of finite syntax) long before consistent past tense -ed {insert\_element\_32\_}.  
  - French/German L2 learners: Avoided finite verbs in nonfinite positions (proving abstract finiteness knowledge) but used infinitives as defaults {insert\_element\_33\_}.  

#### 4. Prosodic Transfer Hypothesis (PTH)  
- **Claim**: L1 prosodic structure interferes with L2 morphology production (e.g., Mandarin lacks English’s prosodic structure for regular past tense -ed) {insert\_element\_34\_}.  
- **Example**: English regular past tense -ed is "adjoined to the prosodic word," while Mandarin aspect markers (e.g., *le*) are inside the prosodic word. Mandarin learners struggle more with English regular -ed than irregular past tense (e.g., *kept*, which matches Mandarin’s prosodic structure) {insert\_element\_35\_}.  


### 7.5 Feature Reassembly Hypothesis (FRH)  
Lardiere’s (2009a,b) FRH explains the hardest L2 morphology challenge: **form-meaning mismatches across languages** {insert\_element\_36\_}.  

#### 1. Core Idea  
Languages bundle formal features (phonological, syntactic, semantic) differently. L2 learners must "reassemble" L1 feature bundles into L2 configurations {insert\_element\_37\_}.  

#### 2. Example: English Past Tense (-ed)  
- English -ed encodes multiple meanings: [+past], perfective aspect (e.g., *devoured* = complete event), politeness (e.g., *I was wondering*), and ambiguity (e.g., *Jane said Joyce was pregnant* = past or present) {insert\_element\_38\_}.  
- Mandarin has no overt past tense—learners must reassemble [past] from context (L1) to a morpheme (L2) that carries extra meanings {insert\_element\_39\_}.  

#### 3. Cross-Linguistic Mismatches  
- Irish encodes tense on complementizers (e.g., *gur* = past complementizer), while Somali encodes past on nouns (e.g., *árday-gii* = "former student") {insert\_element\_40\_}.  
- Learners must adjust not just *which morpheme* to use, but *which lexical category* (verb vs. complementizer vs. noun) carries the feature {insert\_element\_41\_}.  


### 7.6 Conclusions & Teaching Relevance  
1. **Production ≠ Representation**: Omitted/overused morphology (e.g., -s/-ed) often reflects mapping/access issues (MSIH/PTH), not lack of syntax knowledge {insert\_element\_42\_}.  
2. **FRH Explains Hardest Cases**: Form-meaning mismatches (e.g., English past tense) require explicit focus on feature bundles, not just morpheme drills {insert\_element\_43\_}.  
3. **Teaching Tip**: Prioritize "form + meaning + syntax" practice (e.g., linking -ed to both past time and perfective aspect) and build automaticity for lexical access {insert\_element\_44\_}.

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Variability ≠ ignorance**: Learners can **know** a feature (e.g., 3sg agreement) yet **omit** or mis-realize its **surface exponent** under pressure.
- **MSIH**: Representations are intact; variability is at the **spell-out**/phonological mapping stage → predicts better **comprehension** than **production**.
- **PTH**: L1 **prosodic templates** affect where/when morphology can surface → predicts **position-specific** or **syllable/foot**-sensitive omissions.
- **Feature Reassembly (FRH)**: L2 requires **reconfiguring** features and their **bundling** (e.g., definiteness, number, gender) → predicts **asymmetric success** across features and languages, even with the same “category label.”
- **Diagnostics**: Contrast **form-identical** vs. **feature-identical** items; compare **comprehension** (AJT, priming) vs. **production** (elicitation) to locate the bottleneck.

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1, 45 min)

#### 1) What “lives” in morphology and why it’s hard
- **Key notes**: Agreement (person/number), tense/aspect, case, gender—small forms, big feature load; low perceptual salience; late-acquired and low-frequency combinations are especially vulnerable.
- **Class spark**: 2-minute poll—Which morphology caused you most trouble in an L2 (and in what context)?

#### 2) Three explanations for variability
- **MSIH (surface realization bottleneck)**  
  - **Prediction**: Near-native **interpretation** of tense/agreement contrasts; **omissions/mis-formations** in **production**—especially under speed/complexity.
- **PTH (prosodic constraints on realization)**  
  - **Prediction**: Omissions tied to **prosodic position** (e.g., word-final weak syllables) or clitic placement patterns; improvement when prosody favors overt marking.
- **FRH (feature (re)mapping)**  
  - **Prediction**: Difficulty where L2 requires **new feature bundles** or **different feature–form mappings** (e.g., gender on determiners/adjectives; article definiteness for bare-N languages).

#### 3) How to tell them apart (design heuristics)
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Comprehension vs. production** asymmetry → favors **MSIH**.  
  - **Position/syllable effects** → favors **PTH**.  
  - **Feature-specific cross-language asymmetries** (same form, different features) → favors **FRH**.  
- **Quick demo**: Judge vs. produce minimal pairs for 3sg -s, past -ed, or gender agreement; note where breakdown occurs.
