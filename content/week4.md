## Week 4 — Age, Input & the Critical Period

**Anchor text**: Slabakova, R. (2016). *Second Language Acquisition*. **Chapter 4: The Critical Period Hypothesis**.    
 [Link to Chapter 4](/SLA4pg/pdfs/C4_CPH.pdf)






### ⭐ Overview
- What the **Critical Period Hypothesis (CPH)** claims and why age might matter for language learning.  
- How **age of acquisition (AoA)** interacts with **input amount/quality**, **use**, and **education**.  
- Why age effects differ across subsystems: **phonology** (accent) vs. **morphosyntax/semantics** (mixed evidence).  
- What counts as persuasive **evidence** (tasks, outcomes, controls) and what would **falsify** a strong CPH claim.

---

### 🎯 Learning goals
By the end of Week 4 you should be able to:
1. Explain the **basic idea** of a critical/sensitive period for L2 and the kinds of evidence used to test it.  
2. Distinguish **AoA** from other predictors (length of residence, input quality, education, use).  
3. Describe why **phonology** typically shows stronger age effects than **morphosyntax/semantics**.  
4. Write one **if–then prediction** about age and outcomes, and say what result would **challenge** that prediction.

### 🧠 Key terms
*critical period; sensitive period; AoA (age of acquisition); length of residence (LoR); input amount/quality; ultimate attainment; near-native; confound; phonology vs. morphosyntax; aptitude; practice/use; outcome measures*

---

### 🧩 Core content (gentle, big-picture)

#### 1) What the CPH says (and softer variants)
- **Strong (hard) CPH**: after a specific age window, **native-like** attainment is no longer possible.  
- **Sensitive-period view**: age effects are **gradual**; later starters can improve a lot, but **probability** of fully native-like outcomes declines with AoA.  
- **Friendly takeaway**: Age matters, but **how** it matters depends on *what you measure* and *how carefully you measure it*.

#### 2) What outcomes are we talking about?
- **Phonology (accent)**: shows **robust age effects**—late starters often retain a detectable accent even with long residence.  
- **Morphosyntax/semantics**: more **mixed**; late near-natives exist, especially with high-quality input, education, and deliberate practice.  
- **Pragmatics/discourse**: can be challenging well past childhood due to **context-dependent** mappings and lower salience.

#### 3) Measuring “ultimate attainment” (why methods matter)
- **Tasks**: global ratings (accent/intelligibility), **grammaticality judgments**, sentence interpretation, timed processing measures.  
- **Baselines**: who counts as a “native” control? Are items **balanced** and **independent** of vocabulary/background knowledge?  
- **Confounds**: **education**, **socioeconomic factors**, **exposure**, **use**, **motivation**, **aptitude**—all can inflate or mask age effects.  
- **Friendly rule**: the stronger the design (clean baselines, good controls, reliable measures), the **clearer** the age pattern you can interpret.

#### 4) AoA versus input/use (don’t mix them up)
- **AoA** is not the same as **time in the L2**. Two late starters with the same AoA can diverge if one gets **richer input**, **more use**, or **formal schooling**.  
- **Prediction**: Under matched input/education, age effects will still appear—especially in **phonology**—but may be **smaller** in some **morphosyntactic** domains.

#### 5) Shapes of the function (what curves look like)
- **Step-like** (hard CPH): sharp drop after a boundary (e.g., puberty).  
- **Continuous** (sensitive period): gradual decline with **no sharp cliff**.  
- **Hybrid**: steep early decline that **flattens** later—common in real datasets.

#### 6) Why subsystems differ
- **Phonology** depends on early-tuned **perceptual categories** and fine **motor patterns** → strong age effects.  
- **Morphosyntax/semantics** can be **explicitly taught**, are **rehearsable**, and benefit from **literacy** and **education** → more late success cases.  
- **Interfaces** (structure + context) add **processing load**, which can exacerbate age effects when **time pressure** is high.

#### 7) What would count as evidence—for or against?
- **Supports CPH**: a **monotonic AoA effect** remains after controlling input/use/education; late starters cluster **below** native range on demanding measures.  
- **Challenges CPH**: **documented late near-natives** meeting **strict performance criteria**; AoA slope weakens or disappears once **confounds** are rigorously controlled.  
- **Falsifier (hard CPH)**: many late starters consistently within **native distribution** on multiple **independent** measures.

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Not one outcome, many**: Age relates differently to **phonology** (large age effects), **morphosyntax** (mixed), and **semantics/pragmatics** (often learnable with input).
- **Input matters**: The **amount** (quantity) and **informativeness** (quality/diagnosticity) of input can **attenuate** age effects; poor or ambiguous input slows attainment.
- **Operationalization is destiny**: “Nativelikeness” depends on **what you measure** (accent ≠ grammar ≠ processing). Global ratings and local diagnostics can diverge.
- **Design before verdict**: Strong claims (e.g., maturational closing) require **controls** for use, education, SES, literacy, motivation, and **task demands**.

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1, 45 min)

#### 1) What do Critical/Sensitive Period claims predict?
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Strong CPH**: maturational window closes → **discontinuity** in outcomes by AoA.  
  - **Sensitive period**: **continuous** decline with age, but learning remains possible.  
  - **Predictions**: later AoA → lower ultimate attainment **holding input constant**; the shape (break vs. slope) is empirical.
- **Class spark**: One-minute write: *Which skill in your L2 feels most “age-locked”? Why?*

#### 2) Evidence patterns & measurement choices
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Phonology** typically shows robust age effects; **morphosyntax** shows mixed results depending on measure (accuracy vs. online processing).  
  - **Global vs. local**: “Sounds native” ≠ “uses inflection/article semantics native-like.”  
  - **Task effects**: time pressure (online) reveals processing costs despite near-native **offline** accuracy.
- **Think–pair–share**: Identify a study conclusion that would **flip** if a different measure were used.

#### 3) Input quantity/quality and design controls
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Quantity** (hours/years, richness of exposure) and **quality** (diagnostic cues, literacy, classroom focus on form) can **mediate** age effects.  
  - Control for **LoR, current use, education**, and **dominance**; report **exposure profiles**.
- **Quick design pitch**: In pairs, propose **one control** you’d add to an AoA study and say why it matters.

> **Pacing note**: Three topics x ~12 min each + 9 min discussion fits a 45-min session.

### 🧪 Methods micro-lab (Session 2, 45 min)
**Goal**: Separate **age** from **input** in a simple, testable design.

1) **Define outcome** (choose ONE):  
   - Global accent rating • inflectional morphology accuracy • article semantics • scalar implicature judgments.

2) **Groups** (match on input where possible):  
   - Early/AoA≤7 vs. Late/AoA≥15; **match** LoR, current L2 use, education.

3) **Prediction** (pick the right shape):  
   - **Discontinuity** at a boundary (strong CPH) **or** **gradual slope** (sensitive period).  
   - Specify which **module** should show the effect (e.g., phonology > morphosyntax).

4) **Measures** & **controls**:  
   - Outcome measure + at least **two** covariates (LoR, use, literacy).  
   - Plan **power** (rough N) and **analysis** (e.g., regression with AoA, covariates).

5) **Report** (2–3 lines):  
   - Hypothesis, key prediction, primary measure, and what result would **falsify** your claim.
