## What Is This Chapter About?

In earlier chapters, we focused on **what** L2 learners know about grammar (representations).  
This chapter shifts to **how** L2 learners use that knowledge in real time (processing).

> **Big question:** Do L2 learners process language in the same way as native speakers, or in a fundamentally different way?

To answer this, researchers use methods like:
- self-paced reading
- brain recordings (ERPs)
- eye tracking

---

## Two Student Presentations

### 1. Shallow Structure Hypothesis (Clahsen & Felser, 2006)

**Core claim:**  
L2 learners often rely on **shallow**, meaning-based cues rather than building detailed syntactic structures.

**What does “shallow” mean?**  
- Using word meaning, plausibility, and simple word order (e.g., Agent–Verb–Theme)  
- Not fully using abstract syntax (e.g., movement traces, hierarchical structure)

**Example:**  
> The nurse who the doctor argued that the rude patient had angered ___ is refusing to work late.

Native speakers show evidence that they actively “fill the gap” (the missing object of *angered*). According to the Shallow Structure Hypothesis, L2 learners may not process this gap in the same way – they just get the meaning without the detailed syntax.

**What the hypothesis does NOT say:**  
- L2 learners cannot understand complex sentences.  
- L2 learners have no grammar.

**What it does say:**  
- L2 learners process **differently**, not just slower.  
- This supports the idea of a **qualitative difference** between L1 and L2 processing.

**But there is counter-evidence:**  
- Many studies show L2 learners **can** use syntactic constraints (e.g., island effects).  
- **Experience matters**: Learners with naturalistic exposure (immersion) process more like natives.  
- Some native speakers also use shallow processing (e.g., low-literacy readers).

**Takeaway:** The Shallow Structure Hypothesis is a strong claim, but it is not the final word. Proficiency, exposure, and task demands all matter.

---

### 2. Plausibility in L2 Processing (Roberts & Felser, 2011)

**Core question:**  
Do L2 learners use **plausibility** (what makes sense in the real world) during sentence comprehension?

**Garden-path sentences** are used to test this.

Example:

1. *The journalist wrote the book had amazed all the judges.*  
2. *The journalist wrote the girl had amazed all the judges.*

- At first, readers think: “The journalist wrote the book” (plausible) or “The journalist wrote the girl” (implausible).  
- Then the word *had amazed* appears, and the reader must **reanalyze**: “the book / the girl had amazed all the judges.”

**Prediction:** If the initial interpretation is plausible, it is **harder to abandon** than if it is implausible.

**What Roberts & Felser found:**  
- Native speakers and L2 learners both showed this plausibility effect – they took longer to reanalyze when the first reading was plausible.  
- **But:** L2 learners struggled more with complex structures (e.g., adjunct clauses like *While the band played the song…*).

**Conclusion:**  
L2 learners do use plausibility, just like natives. However, structural complexity can still cause extra difficulty.

## Key Concepts You Need to Know

| Concept | What it means |
|---------|----------------|
| **Real-time processing** | Understanding language as it arrives, moment by moment |
| **Incremental processing** | Building interpretation word by word, not waiting until the sentence ends |
| **Garden-path sentence** | A sentence that tricks you into a wrong initial analysis |
| **Reanalysis** | Revising your initial interpretation when new words show it was wrong |
| **Plausibility** | Whether an interpretation makes sense based on world knowledge |
| **Shallow vs. deep parsing** | Shallow = meaning-based; deep = full syntactic structure |
| **Representation vs. processing** | Representation = what you know; processing = how you use it online |

## Research Methods (Briefly)

### Self-paced reading
- You press a button to see each word.  
- Longer reading time = processing difficulty.

### ERPs (event-related potentials)
- Electrodes on the scalp measure brain activity.  
- **N400** (~400 ms) = semantic difficulty.  
- **P600** (~600 ms) = syntactic difficulty or reanalysis.  
- **LAN** (left anterior negativity) = early morphosyntactic processing (less reliable).

### Eye tracking
- Tracks where you look while reading.  
- **Fixations** = pauses.  
- **Regressions** = looking back (sign of trouble).

---

## What About Individual Differences?

L2 learners are **not a homogeneous group**. Neither are native speakers.

Factors that affect processing:
- **Proficiency** – higher proficiency → more native-like patterns
- **Reading speed** – if L2 readers match native speed, differences often disappear
- **Working memory** – smaller effect than you might think
- **Experience** – immersion helps
- **Cognitive control** – helps with reanalysis and switching between interpretations
- **Task demands** – paying attention to grammar changes results

**Important:**  
> Slower processing ≠ different grammar.  
> A processing difficulty is not automatically a competence deficit.

---

## Summary: Two Positions in L2 Processing

| **Qualitative Difference View** | **Quantitative Similarity / Convergence View** |
|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| L2 processing is different in kind | L2 processing is basically the same, but slower/less automatic |
| Supported by: Shallow Structure Hypothesis | Supported by: Full Transfer/Full Access, Fundamental Similarity Hypothesis |
| Prediction: Nativelike processing is impossible | Prediction: With enough proficiency/experience, processing converges |

**Current evidence leans toward the similarity view**, but with important caveats:  
- L2 processing is more vulnerable under complexity.  
- L2 learners benefit from **attention to form** and **rich input**.

# Study Guide: L2 Processing (Chapter 12)

## What This Chapter Is About

This chapter explores how second language (L2) speakers process language in real time. Researchers want to know: **Do L2 learners use the same mental processes as native speakers, or are they fundamentally different?**

We will look at:
- The main experimental methods used to study processing
- Theoretical debates (difference vs. similarity)
- How L2 speakers handle grammar, word meanings, and sentence structure
- Why individual differences matter (memory, speed, experience)

---

## 12.1 How Do Researchers Study L2 Processing?

Scientists use several techniques to "see" what happens in the mind/brain during language comprehension.

### Self-Paced Reading (Moving Window)
- You see one word at a time, pressing a button for the next word.
- Longer reading times = more processing difficulty.
- Example: Garden-path sentences (e.g., *The horse raced past the barn fell*) cause longer pauses because you have to re-analyze.

### Event-Related Potentials (ERPs)
- Measures brain’s electrical activity (via electrodes on the scalp) with millisecond precision.
- Two important brain responses:

| Response | When it happens | What it means |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| **N400** | ~400 ms after a word | Difficulty integrating meaning; unexpected or odd words |
| **P600** | ~500–600 ms after a word | Syntactic problem; garden-path reanalysis; grammar errors |

- **LAN (Left Anterior Negativity)** – earlier response to grammar errors (less reliable).

### Eye-Tracking
- Tracks where you look while reading or listening.
- **Fixations** = pauses on a word.
- **Regressions** = looking back (sign of processing trouble).
- **Early measures** (first fixation) vs. **late measures** (total time, rereading).

> **Important**: These responses are not unique to language – they also appear with music and pictures.

---

## 12.2 Two Opposing Views on L2 Processing

Researchers disagree about whether L2 processing is qualitatively different from L1 processing.

| **Difference View** | **Similarity View** |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Late learners use different brain systems | Same mechanisms; only quantitative differences |
| L2 processing is shallow (meaning-based, not structural) | With enough proficiency/experience, L2 processing converges with L1 |
| Nativelike attainment is impossible | Nativelike processing is possible |
| Key models: Shallow Structure Hypothesis (SSH), Declarative/Procedural model | Key models: Full Transfer/Full Access, Fundamental Similarity Hypothesis, Convergence Hypothesis |

> **Your task**: As you read the evidence, decide which view you find more convincing.

---

## 12.3 Processing of Grammar: Functional Morphology

### Early Studies (e.g., Weber-Fox & Neville 1996)
- Late L2 learners showed no LAN or P600 for grammar errors → argued for Critical Period Hypothesis.
- **Problem**: Age of arrival was mixed up with proficiency – earlier learners were simply more proficient.

### Later Studies (better controlled)
- **High-proficiency learners** can show native-like LAN+P600 patterns (Rossi et al. 2006).
- **Distance matters**: Local agreement (e.g., *a small house*) is easier than long-distance agreement (e.g., *the house is small* – noun and adjective far apart). L2 learners struggle more with distance.
- **L1 transfer**: Chinese learners of Spanish (Chinese has almost no inflection) showed only P600, not LAN – their brain couldn't produce the early negativity.

### Eye-tracking evidence (Keating 2009)
- Learners were sensitive to noun-adjective agreement, but they needed more time and regressions, especially when words were far apart.

### Takeaway
- L2 learners **can** acquire inflectional morphology.
- Their processing may be less automatic and more effortful, but it's not impossible.

---

## 12.4 Parsing and Syntactic Processing

### What is the "parser"?
- The mental system that builds sentence structure as you read or listen.
- It works incrementally (word by word).

### Native Processing: Two Routes (LAST model)
- **Route 1 (fast & shallow)**: Uses word meanings, common patterns, plausibility. Works ~85% of the time.
- **Route 2 (slow & deep)**: Uses full grammar, movement traces, hierarchical structure.

### Shallow Structure Hypothesis (SSH) – Clahsen & Felser
- Claims L2 learners **only** have the shallow route.
- Evidence: L2 learners don't seem to notice "invisible" copies of moved words (wh-traces) in long-distance questions.

### Counter-evidence (against SSH)
- Many studies show L2 learners **do** respect grammatical constraints (islands, wh-movement).
- **Experience matters**: Learners who studied abroad (immersion) process wh-traces like natives; classroom learners do not.
- **Native speakers also use shallow processing** sometimes – especially those with lower education or less reading experience.

### Takeaway
- The Shallow Structure Hypothesis is controversial. Most recent evidence suggests L2 learners **can** build deep syntactic representations, especially if they have enough naturalistic experience.

---

## 12.5 Integrating Meaning into Sentence Processing

### Garden-Path Sentences
- Example: *After Bill drank the water proved to be poisonous.*
- You first think "Bill drank the water" (makes sense), then you see "proved" and realize "the water" is actually the subject of "proved". You must re-analyze (garden path).
- This reanalysis takes time and requires knowing verb properties (e.g., *drink* can be transitive or intransitive).

### Key Findings
- **L2 learners are good at using verb information** (subcategorization, biases) – they show slower reading at the disambiguating word.
- **Plausibility matters**: If the first interpretation is plausible (e.g., *wrote the book*), it's harder to abandon than an implausible one (e.g., *wrote the girl*). Both natives and L2 learners show this effect.
- **But**: Complex structures (adjunct clauses like *While the band played...*) cause more difficulty for L2 learners.

### Takeaway
- Lexico-semantic (meaning) integration is easier for L2 learners than grammar processing.
- L2 learners can process meaning incrementally, but structural complexity can slow them down.

---

## 12.6 Individual Differences: Not All Learners Are the Same

### Factors That Affect L2 Processing

| Factor | What it means |
|--------|----------------|
| **Proficiency** | Higher proficiency → more nativelike patterns (especially when attention is on grammar) |
| **Processing speed** | If L2 readers match native reading speed, group differences disappear |
| **Working memory** | Small effect; explains little variance |
| **Linguistic experience** | The more exposure, the easier processing becomes |
| **Cognitive control** | Helps with reanalysis and switching between interpretations |
| **Resource allocation** | Some learners focus on form, others on meaning – this varies |

### Surprising Finding
- **Native speakers also vary widely**.
- Tanner & van Hell (2014): Individual native speakers' brain responses to grammar errors ranged continuously between N400 and P600 – not a single "native pattern".
- Therefore, L2 variation may simply reflect universal variation among **all** language users.

### Takeaway
- Avoid saying "L2 learners do X" – they are not a homogeneous group.
- Future research must always consider individual differences.

---

## 12.7 Conclusion

The debate continues, but most evidence now suggests:

- L2 processing mechanisms are **qualitatively similar** to L1.
- Differences are **quantitative**: slower, more effortful, more dependent on experience and individual factors.
- Apparent differences may be due to processing pressures, not faulty underlying grammar.

> **Key insight**: L2 learners can achieve nativelike processing, especially with immersion, high proficiency, and when their attention is directed to grammatical form.
