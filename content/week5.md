## CHAPTER 5: FIRST LANGUAGE ACQUISITION & BILINGUAL FIRST LANGUAGE (2L1)  
**Core Goal**: Understand how children build L1 skills (sounds to syntax) and how 2L1 learners develop two languages simultaneously—with focus on universal patterns and language-specific variation.  


### 5.1 Acquisition of the Sounds of L1  
Children master 4 foundational sound-related skills to build a phonological system:  
1. **Phoneme Categorization**:  
   - Infants (6–8 months) distinguish *all* human speech sounds (e.g., Hindi dental vs. retroflex /d/), but by 10–12 months, they "prune" non-native contrasts (English infants lose Hindi /d/ distinction) {insert\_element\_0\_}.  
   - Measured via the **high-amplitude sucking (HAS) paradigm**: Infants dishabituate (suck harder) to new sounds, showing they detect differences {insert\_element\_1\_}.  

2. **Speech Segmentation**:  
   - Challenge: Speech has no "spaces" between words (unlike writing).  
   - Cues used:  
     - Isolated words (6–60 caregiver words/hour are spoken alone, e.g., "Daddy") {insert\_element\_2\_}.  
     - Prosody (e.g., English 90% of 2-syllable words use "STRONG-weak" stress: *baby*) via the **prosodic bootstrapping hypothesis** {insert\_element\_3\_}.  
     - Phonotactics (language-specific sound combinations: English forbids /tl/ at syllable start; Bulgarian allows it) {insert\_element\_4\_}.  

3. **Phonological Production**:  
   - Children simplify adult sounds (e.g., [gak] for "clock," [deks] for "desk") but recognize unpronounceable words—proving their sound representations match adults {insert\_element\_5\_}.  


### 5.2 Learning Word Meanings  
Word learning follows a slow-start, fast-growth pattern, with strategies to resolve ambiguity:  
1. **Milestones**:  
   - 1st word: ~12 months.  
   - 50 words: ~18–20 months (slow phase).  
   - **Vocabulary spurt**: 1–2 words/day after 20 months; 10 words/day (ages 2–6); 6-year-olds know ~14,000 words {insert\_element\_6\_}.  

2. **Solving the Gavagai Problem** (ambiguous word meanings):  
   - **Whole Object Assumption**: New words label whole objects (not parts, e.g., "gavagai" = rabbit, not tail) {insert\_element\_7\_}.  
   - **Mutual Exclusivity**: No two words share the same meaning (e.g., if "spoon" is known, "fendle" = unknown whisk) {insert\_element\_8\_}.  
   - **Syntactic Bootstrapping**: Use sentence structure to guess verb meanings (e.g., "Mary clessed X to Y" → *cless* = 3-participant action like "give") {insert\_element\_9\_}.  

3. **Verb Learning Challenges**:  
   - Verbs require learning "event structure" (e.g., *eat* needs an animate Agent + edible Theme). Children overextend transitive frames (e.g., "Don’t giggle me") due to frequent transitive input {insert\_element\_10\_}.  


### 5.3 Acquisition of Functional Morphology & Syntax  
Children learn grammatical markers and sentence structure in predictable, stage-based ways:  
1. **Functional Morphology**:  
   - **Regular Inflections**: Learned via rules (e.g., plural -s, past -ed) — the **Wug Test** (Berko Gleason 1958) shows 4–5 year-olds produce "wugs" 75% accurately {insert\_element\_11\_}.  
   - **Irregular Forms**: Follow a **U-shaped curve**: Correct use (e.g., "went") → overregularization (e.g., "goed") → correct use again (rules vs. memorization competition) {insert\_element\_12\_}.  

2. **Syntax Development**:  
   - **Stages**: 1-word (e.g., "Milk!") → 2-word (e.g., "Mommy fix") → multi-word.  
   - **MLU (Mean Length of Utterance)**: Measures syntactic growth (count morphemes per 100 utterances). Eve reached MLU=4 by 30 months; Adam/Sarah’s growth was slower {insert\_element\_13\_}.  
   - **Complex Structures**: Object wh-questions (e.g., *Who did Mary help?*) are harder than subject questions—they require crossing more syntactic nodes (structural complexity) {insert\_element\_14\_}.  

3. **Cross-Linguistic Variation**:  
   - Languages without tense morphology (e.g., Mandarin) use context/time adverbials to signal temporality. Mandarin children master this by age 3;2 (later than English tense learning) {insert\_element\_15\_}.  


### 5.4 Acquisition of Semantics & Pragmatics  
Subtle, context-dependent meanings take longer to master than surface forms:  
1. **Imperfective Aspect**:  
   - 3–5 year-olds allow imperfective forms (e.g., Italian *comia*, Dutch progressive) to refer to completed events (adults do not) — they lack discourse-time anchoring {insert\_element\_16\_}.  

2. **Scalar Implicatures**:  
   - Sentences like "Some horses jumped" are logically true (if all jumped) but pragmatically infelicitous. 5-year-olds reject this only 12.5% of the time (adults: 92.5%)—they struggle comparing speaker vs. hearer perspectives {insert\_element\_17\_}.  

3. **Key Factor**: Computational complexity (holding two perspectives in working memory) delays mastery {insert\_element\_18\_}.  


### 5.5 Bilingual First Language (2L1) Acquisition  
Simultaneous bilinguals (L1+L2 from birth) build two separate but interacting grammars:  
1. **Evidence for Independent Systems**:  
   - Early Separation: 6–8 month-olds distinguish their two languages (e.g., French-Spanish rhythm differences) {insert\_element\_19\_}.  
   - Same Stages as Monolinguals: Pass through 1-word → 2-word stages; make similar errors (overregularization) {insert\_element\_20\_}.  
   - Input Dominance: More exposure to Language A → stronger skills in A (e.g., Spanish-English kids with 70% Spanish input have stronger Spanish) {insert\_element\_21\_}.  

2. **Cross-Linguistic Influence**:  
   - Occurs in constrained areas:  
     - **Syntax-discourse interface**: English-Italian 2L1 learners overuse overt subjects in Italian (a null-subject language) {insert\_element\_22\_}.  
     - **Processing Economy**: French-Dutch kids use Dutch wh-in-situ (allowed in French, simpler than Dutch wh-movement) {insert\_element\_23\_}.  


### 5.6 Conclusions  
1. **L1 Acquisition Is Innate + Input-Driven**: Infants use innate sensitivity (phoneme detection) and input cues (prosody) to master sounds, words, and grammar.  
2. **Complexity Delays Mastery**: Structural complexity (object wh-questions) and computational complexity (scalar implicatures) are mastered by 7–8 years, not 4.  
3. **2L1 Learners Are Unique**: They separate languages early but show constrained influence—comparing them to monolinguals is unfair.

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Context matters**: 2L1 receives **early, rich, balanced** input in both languages; Adult L2 begins **late, often classroom-heavy**; Heritage learners have **early naturalistic input** but later **reduced input** and **dominance shift**; Attriters show **loss/weakening** tied to **reduced use**.
- **Predicted strengths/weaknesses**:
  - **2L1**: native-like developmental path; occasional crosslinguistic influence but robust end-state when input is sufficient.
  - **Adult L2**: vulnerabilities in **functional morphology** and some **interfaces**; stronger role for **transfer** and **processing pressure**.
  - **Heritage**: strong **phonology/lexicon**; selective vulnerabilities in **morphology** and **discourse-conditioned syntax** (e.g., subject expression, clitics) due to input profile.
  - **Attrition**: erosion in **low-frequency**/late-acquired morpho-syntax and **discourse mapping**, especially under limited use.
- **Diagnosing source**: Distinguish **incomplete acquisition** (never fully robust) vs. **attrition** (previously acquired, later weakened) via **age-of-onset**, **input history**, **use**, and **change over time**.
