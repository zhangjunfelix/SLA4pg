## CHAPTER 10: ACQUISITION OF THE SYNTAX–SEMANTICS INTERFACE  
**Core Goal**: Understand how L2 learners map syntactic forms to semantic meanings—especially when L1 and L2 differ in form–meaning pairings, and when input is insufficient for learning.  


### 10.1 Types of Meaning in Language  
Meaning is not a single construct—it arises from five interacting layers, all shaped by syntax:  

| Type of Meaning | Definition | Example |
|------------------|------------|---------|
| **Lexical** | Literal, idiosyncratic meaning of words (stored in the mental lexicon) | English *cat* = "small furry animal"; Italian *gatto* = same denotation . |
| **Phrasal (Sentential) Semantic** | Compositional meaning (combines word meanings + syntax/word order) | English *Cats were exterminated* = 2 meanings ("some cats" or "all cats"); Italian *Gatti sono stati sterminati* = only "some cats" . |
| **Grammatical** | Meaning encoded in inflectional morphology (tense, aspect, agreement) | *eats* = present tense + habitual; *ate* = past tense (completed/habitual) . |
| **Discourse-Related** | Meaning dependent on prior context (e.g., topic/focus) | English topicalization: *Sushi, she eats* = "we’re talking about sushi" . |
| **Pragmatic** | Implied meaning (not overtly encoded) | *Bonnie ate some strawberries* = implicates "not all strawberries" (Scalar Implicature) . |

- **Key Principle**: Semantics is computed first (context-free), then pragmatics adjusts meaning (context-dependent)—"semantics proposes, pragmatics disposes" .  


### 10.2 Syntax–Semantics Mismatches  
All languages convey the same universal meanings, but they use different forms to encode them. These mismatches are L2 acquisition hotspots:  

#### 1. Example 1: Aspectual Tenses (English vs. Spanish)  
- **English**:  
  - Past progressive (*was robbing*) = only ongoing events .  
  - Simple past (*robbed*) = completed OR habitual events .  
- **Spanish**:  
  - Imperfect (*robaba*) = ongoing OR habitual events .  
  - Preterit (*robó*) = only completed events .  
- **Mismatch**: The same "habitual" meaning is encoded by simple past (English) vs. Imperfect (Spanish); "ongoing" = past progressive (English) vs. Imperfect (Spanish) .  

#### 2. Example 2: Generic NPs (English vs. Spanish)  
- **English**: Bare plurals (*Tigers eat meat*) = generic; definite plurals (*The tigers eat meat*) = specific .  
- **Spanish**: Definite plurals (*Los tigres comen carne*) = generic OR specific; bare plurals (*Tigres comen carne*) = ungrammatical .  
- **Mismatch**: Generic meaning uses bare plurals (English) vs. definite plurals (Spanish) .  

#### 3. Acquisition Pattern  
- Learners initially transfer L1 form–meaning mappings (e.g., Spanish learners of English overuse simple past for habitual events) .  
- Advanced learners restructure to L2 mappings (e.g., Bulgarian learners of English master that bare verbs = completed events: *I saw Mary cross* ≠ *I saw Mary crossing* ) .  


### 10.3 Poverty of the Stimulus (POS) in Semantics  
POS occurs when learners acquire knowledge not present in input (no negative evidence for missing meanings). UG constrains hypotheses to solve this:  

#### 1. Example 1: Spanish Impersonal *se*  
- **Spanish Imperfect (*se comía*)**: *se* = generic ("one eats") OR specific ("we ate") .  
- **Spanish Preterit (*se comió*)**: *se* = only specific ("we ate") (no generic reading) .  
- **POS Challenge**: Learners never hear *se comió* used to mean "one ate"—they must infer this meaning is impossible .  
- **Finding**: Advanced learners reject the generic reading of preterit *se* 85% accurately .  

#### 2. Example 2: Japanese Quantifier Scope  
- **Japanese SOV (*Dareka-ga dono hon-mo yonda*)**: Only 1 meaning ("someone read every book") .  
- **Japanese OSV (scrambled: *Dono hon-mo dareka-ga yonda*)**: 2 meanings ("someone read every book" OR "for each book, someone read it") .  
- **POS Challenge**: Learners never hear SOV sentences with the second meaning—they must infer it is missing .  
- **Finding**: Half of advanced English learners of Japanese correctly reject the missing meaning, proving UG access .  


### 10.4 Novel Constructions  
When L2 has a syntactic construction absent in L1, learners acquire its syntax first—semantics follows automatically via universal computation:  

#### Example: French Discontinuous Interrogatives  
- **Continuous**: *Qui de célèbre fumait?* ("Which famous person smoked?") = 2 meanings (past OR present celebrity) .  
- **Discontinuous**: *Qui fumait de célèbre?* ("Which famous person smoked?") = only 1 meaning (past celebrity) .  
- **Acquisition**:  
  - Learners first master that French allows discontinuous *wh*-phrases (syntax) .  
  - Semantics follows: They automatically infer the missing present-celebrity meaning (no input needed) .  
- **Finding**: Even intermediate learners (3 semesters of French) distinguish the two constructions’ meanings .  


### 10.5 Acquiring Morphologically Unmarked Meanings: English Articles  
Languages without articles (Russian, Korean) force learners to map abstract semantic features (definiteness/specificity) to new morphology:  

#### The Article Choice Parameter (Ionin et al. 2004)  
- **Parameter Settings**:  
  - **Definiteness (English)**: *the* = definite, *a* = indefinite .  
  - **Specificity (Samoan)**: Articles distinguish specific vs. nonspecific (regardless of definiteness) .  
- **Learner Behavior**:  
  - Russian/Korean learners fluctuate between settings: Overuse *the* with indefinite specific NPs (e.g., "I want to sell the necklace") and *a* with definite nonspecific NPs .  
  - Advanced learners fix to the target (definiteness) with sufficient input .  

#### Key Challenge  
- Definiteness/specificity are universal concepts, but their morphological expression is new. Learners need input to "choose" which feature English articles encode .  


### 10.6 Conclusions  
1. **Meaning Layers Interact**: Syntax shapes all meaning types—mismatches between L1/L2 form–meaning pairs cause the most difficulty .  
2. **UG Solves POS**: Semantic knowledge (e.g., missing interpretations) cannot come from input alone—UG constrains hypotheses .  
3. **Syntax Precedes Semantics**: For novel constructions, acquiring syntax first triggers automatic semantic computation (no need to teach semantics) .  
4. **Abstract Features Are Hard**: Morphologically unmarked meanings (e.g., articles) require mapping abstract features to form—take time but are learnable .

### 🔍 Core ideas
- **Event composition & telicity**: VP meaning arises from verb + object; **quantized** objects (e.g., *an apple*, *three apples*) tend to yield **telic** interpretations (bounded events), while **mass/plural bare** objects (*apples*, *rice*) favor **atelic** readings.
- **Articles & DP features**: Languages package **[±definite]**, **[±specific]**, and **generic** readings differently. Learners from **article-less L1s** must map new features to forms; expect **overuse/underuse** of *the/a* and confusion in **context-dependent** readings.
- **Quantification & scope**: Sentences with multiple quantifiers (e.g., *A student read every book*) can have **surface** (∃ > ∀) and **inverse** (∀ > ∃) readings. L1 grammar and processing preferences shape which readings L2 learners access.

### 🗂️ Mini-lecture outline (Session 1, 45 min)

#### 1) Event structure & telicity
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Accomplishments** (bounded events) vs. **activities** (unbounded) depend on the **internal argument**: *eat an apple* (telic) vs. *eat apples* (atelic).  
  - Adding **measure phrases** (*drink a glass of water*) or **resultative structure** increases boundedness.  
  - Predictions: Learners may misinterpret aspect when the **object’s quantization** or **measure** is the only cue.
- **Class spark**: Label a set of VPs (provided) as telic/atelic and justify by object type/measure.

#### 2) Article semantics: definiteness, specificity, generics
- **Key notes**:  
  - **Definiteness** = unique, familiar referent (*the book*); **specificity** = speaker intends a particular but not necessarily familiar referent (*a certain book*).  
  - **Generics**: *The tiger/The tigers/Tigers are endangered*—form–meaning mapping varies.  
  - L2 predictions: **Article-less L1s** overuse *the* for specificity or familiarity, underuse *a* in novel but non-specific contexts; difficulty with **generic** readings.
- **Micro-demo**: Two-sentence contexts that force **definite vs. indefinite** vs. **specific** readings; students pick the felicitous article.

#### 3) Quantifier scope & ambiguity resolution
- **Key notes**:  
  - Scope options: *A student read every book* (∃ > ∀ vs. ∀ > ∃).  
  - Diagnostics: picture verification, **TVJT** with explicit contexts, or continuation tasks.  
  - L2 predictions: Preference for **surface scope** early; inverse scope access depends on L1, proficiency, and processing load.
- **Think–pair–share**: Draft one **context sentence** that forces **inverse scope** for *A reviewer read every paper*, then test with a minimal prompt.
